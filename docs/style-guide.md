# Bakahan writing voice

Every guide should sound like the same person wrote it: a Filipino who raises cattle, has made mistakes, and is explaining things to a neighbour or a younger relative who wants to start. Practical, calm, a little warm. Not a textbook, not a press release, not a vlogger.

## Voice

- **Talk to the reader, not down to them.** "Weigh every two weeks if you can borrow a scale" reads better than "It is recommended that cattle be weighed regularly."
- **Never invent experience.** No made-up anecdotes, farms, quotes or "raisers we know". Real experience comes only from the owner's own farm notes, and only when they provide them.
- **Be concrete.** Name the thing: *Napier*, *ipil-ipil*, *kural*, *dayami*, *the Municipal Agriculture Office*, *the viajero*, *Padre Garcia on a Friday morning*. Avoid generic phrases like "various factors" and "a range of options".
- **Plain sentences.** Mostly short ones. One idea per sentence. It's fine to start with "But" or "So".
- **Say when something depends.** "Ask your MAO, because rules differ by province" is honest. Don't pretend there's one national answer when there isn't.
- **Numbers carry their source and a hedge:** "about 2.5 to 3% of body weight in dry matter, according to the DA". Never a bare statistic.

## Philippine, not foreign; natural, not try-hard

- Use the words Filipino raisers actually use, even in the English version: *kural*, *viajero*, *paiwi*, *dayami*, *liveweight*, *MAO*, *barangay*, *bakahan*. Explain a term the first time, in passing, then just use it.
- Places, institutions and money are local: pesos (₱), provinces, BAI, DA, PCC, ATI, Landbank, PAGASA, the rainy season and *tag-init*. Don't use American framing like "ranch", "acre" (use hectare), "Fahrenheit" or "county".
- **Don't overdo it.** No forced "Kabayan!", no "mga ka-bakahan", no jokes, no sprinkling of Tagalog words for flavour. One natural local term per paragraph is plenty.
- **Filipino versions** are conversational Tagalog as spoken in Luzon farm towns, with English loanwords where people really use them: *feeds*, *weight*, *kilo*, *silage*, *permit*, *puhunan*, *kita*.
  - Not deep or formal Tagalog: avoid *salapi* for money, *talaan* where *record* is natural, *pagkaing pang-hayop* where *feeds* is natural.
  - Not literal translations of the English. Rewrite the sentence the way a person would say it.
  - Spell English loanwords normally (*silage*, not *saylej*) unless the Filipino spelling is the common one (*bitamina*).

## Things that make writing sound machine-made (avoid all of these)

- Em dashes and en dashes used as punctuation. Use a comma, a full stop or parentheses.
- Headings like "Topic: catchy phrase". Use plain headings: "Choosing the site".
- "Key takeaways", "In conclusion", "In today's…", "Whether you're X or Y", "Let's dive in", "It's important to note that", "plays a crucial role", "navigate", "landscape", "journey", "unlock", "empower", "game-changer", "robust".
- Groups of three adjectives or three parallel clauses for rhythm ("simple, practical and effective").
- Every section the same length, and every list exactly three or five items.
- Bold text in the middle of every paragraph. Bold is only for a word the reader must not miss.
- Ending each section with a one-line moral.
- Emojis, numbered badges, exclamation marks.

## Structure

- 2 to 3 sentence intro, no heading.
- Plain `##` headings in the order a farmer would actually do things.
- At most one table, only when comparing numbers.
- End with `## Summary` (Filipino: `## Buod`): 4 to 6 short bullets.
- Sources listed in the frontmatter, not in the body.
