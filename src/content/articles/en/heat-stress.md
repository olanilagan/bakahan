Cattle suffer from heat long before they look sick. A steer that stands panting in the sun all afternoon eats less and gains less, and in fattening that is money lost. Most of the fixes are cheap: shade, moving air, feeding at the right time and clean water.

## When heat becomes a problem

According to PAGASA, the hot part of the dry season runs from March to May, when temperature and humidity reach their highest levels. That is tag-init, and it is when you need to watch your animals most closely.

El Niño years make it worse. In February 2024 the Pangasinan Provincial Veterinary Office reported cattle with signs of heat stroke, such as heavy drooling and weakness. Follow PAGASA's seasonal outlooks so you can prepare early.

Humidity matters as much as temperature. Cattle cool themselves by sweating and breathing out heat, and both work poorly when the air is humid and still. The North Dakota State University (NDSU) extension service also warns that two or more hot days in a row without cool nights can be deadly, because the animals never get to recover.

## Signs of heat stress

Check your animals in the early afternoon. Watch for:

- Drooling.
- Faster breathing, then panting. By the time cattle pant, some heat stress has already happened.
- Open-mouth breathing with the tongue out. This is serious.
- Eating less, or leaving feed in the trough.
- Standing more and lying down less, or restless.
- Crowding around the water trough or bunching together in the shade.

The University of Wisconsin-Madison Extension gives a simple test: count breaths (watch the flank move) for one full minute. Below about 90 breaths per minute is normal. Between 90 and 110, watch closely. Above 110 the animal is in distress, and above 130 you need to act right away.

Young calves and heavy animals near the end of fattening are most at risk.

## Why Brahman types cope better

Zebu cattle, the humped kind like Brahman and most of our crossbreds, come from hot countries. A 2023 study in the journal Frontiers in Genetics found that cattle with more Brahman blood had larger sweat glands, and sweating is the main way cattle lose heat in hot weather. Zebu cattle also tend to produce less body heat.

That is part of why the DA Region III guide on cattle raising says tropical breeds adapt faster to our climate. But Brahman and crossbred cattle still need shade and water, and animals with more temperate blood need even more care.

## Shade and moving air

Every animal should be able to reach shade during the hottest hours without fighting for it. Wisconsin Extension suggests about 30 square feet of shade per beef cow, which is roughly 3 square metres per head. If the shade is too small, cattle crowd together and trap even more heat.

- Trees are the cheapest shade.
- In a kural (pen) for fattening, a roof of GI sheets or nipa works. DA Region 7 has encouraged raisers to build simple, low-cost cooling sheds from native materials.
- Leave the sides of the shed open so wind can pass through. Moving air does a lot to cool cattle.
- If your cattle are tethered on pasture, move them to a shaded spot before the midday heat.

## Feeding at the cooler times of day

Digesting feed produces heat inside the animal, so feeding at midday adds to the heat load.

- Give the main feeding in the late afternoon or evening, so most of the digestion happens during the cooler night. NDSU recommends a small meal in the morning and a larger one in the afternoon when you feed more than once a day.
- Expect cattle to eat a little less during a heat wave and for a few days after. Don't force concentrate feeds on a panting animal.
- Avoid handling, weighing or loading cattle at midday. Do it early in the morning, in small groups.

## How much water cattle need

Water is the first thing to run short in tag-init. NDSU notes that water needs often double in hot weather. The amounts below are from NDSU at about 32°C, converted from US gallons and rounded. Treat them as approximate. Actual intake depends on the animal, the weather and the feed (fresh grass and silage contain water, dry dayami or rice straw does not).

| Animal | Approximate water per day at about 32°C |
|---|---|
| Growing cattle, about 180 kg | 36 litres |
| Growing cattle, about 270 kg | 48 litres |
| Growing cattle, about 360 kg | 57 litres |
| Finishing cattle, about 450 kg | 78 litres |
| Cow nursing a calf | 68 litres |

Water should always be available, not just once or twice a day. If you carry water by drum, check the troughs again in the early afternoon. NDSU also advises at least 2 inches (about 5 cm) of trough length per animal so timid animals can drink too.

## Keeping water clean

Cattle drink less when water is dirty or warm.

- Keep troughs and drums in the shade. Water in a black drum under the sun gets hot fast.
- Scrub troughs regularly to remove moss, algae, feed and dung.
- Be careful with stagnant ponds in the dry season. University of Nebraska Extension warns that blue-green algae grow fast in warm, still water and can poison cattle. Bright green or blue-green water is a warning sign.
- If water comes from a new or doubtful source, ask your Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) about having it tested.

## Wetting and sprinklers

DA Region 7 has suggested bathing animals from time to time in the hot months. If you use sprinklers or a hose, NDSU advises:

- Use large drops that soak through the hair to the skin. A fine mist can just raise the humidity and make things worse, especially in a closed shed.
- Wet the animals and the ground where they lie, then let the air dry them. That drying is what cools them.
- Start before the hottest part of the day, not when animals are already in trouble.

## Emergencies and when to call the vet

If an animal is breathing with its mouth open, drooling heavily, staggering or lying down and unable to get up:

1. Move it gently to shade where there is moving air. Do not chase or force it.
2. Give it access to cool, clean drinking water.
3. Wet it thoroughly with water. NDSU notes that soaking may be needed to save a badly stressed animal, but avoid cooling it too fast or stressing it further.
4. Call your veterinarian or the MAO right away.

The Negros Occidental Provincial Veterinary Office has recommended electrolytes and vitamins for livestock during extreme heat. Ask your vet which product to use and how much. Report deaths from heat to your Provincial Veterinary Office.

## Summary

- The hottest months are March to May, and El Niño years can make them worse. Prepare before the heat arrives.
- Drooling, panting and open-mouth breathing are signs of heat stress. Count breaths and act early.
- Brahman and zebu types cope better, but all cattle need shade (about 3 square metres each) and moving air.
- Feed mainly in the late afternoon or evening, and don't handle cattle at midday.
- Water should always be available, clean and in the shade. Needs can double in hot weather.
- If an animal collapses or breathes with its mouth open, move it gently to shade, wet it and call the vet.
