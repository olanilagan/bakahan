The trip from your kural to the buyer, the market or the slaughterhouse is where a lot of money quietly disappears. Cattle lose weight on the road, some get hurt during loading, and a truck stopped at a checkpoint without the right papers can sit for hours in the heat. Most of these losses can be avoided with a bit of planning.

## The papers, in short

Our guide on buying and selling cattle covers these in detail. Before an animal leaves your farm, you will usually need:

- A veterinary health certificate from a government veterinarian (your municipal, city or provincial vet).
- A BAI shipping permit if the animals cross provinces or go by boat.
- Proof of ownership and a certificate of transfer if the animal was sold. PD 533, the Anti-Cattle Rustling Law, requires these for large cattle.
- Any permit or fee your town or province requires on top of these.

Requirements and fees differ by province, so ask your MAO or the provincial vet's office a few days ahead, not on the morning of the trip. If you hire a viajero or a trucker who hauls cattle regularly, ask whether they are registered with BAI. BAI Administrative Order No. 08 (2004) covers the registration of livestock handlers and the accreditation of transport carriers.

## What the rules say about animal welfare

The Animal Welfare Act (RA 8485) applies to farm animals, including on the road. Under it, BAI issued Administrative Order No. 19 (2006) on moving live animals by land. The points that matter most for a raiser:

- The owner is responsible for making sure each animal is fit to travel, and someone who knows how to handle cattle must go with the load. The driver can be that person.
- Animals must have suitable feed and enough water for the trip.
- Continuous travel should not go beyond 12 hours, according to the order. Resting places should be planned before you leave.
- Electric prods are not allowed. Cattle must not be lifted or dragged by the head, horns, legs, tail, ears or nose ring, and must not be made to jump down from the truck.
- In warm weather, stops should be short and the truck should park in the shade with as much airflow as possible.

The order also asks the carrier to prepare a written travel plan with the route, loading points and resting places.

## Preparing the animals

Don't load a sick, weak, lame or very heavily pregnant animal. If you're not sure an animal is fit, ask your vet before the trip, not after.

An animal loaded with a belly full of fresh grass or wet feed will scour and urinate heavily, which makes the floor slippery and adds to weight loss. Mississippi State University Extension suggests giving dry feed (hay or dayami, which is rice straw) instead of lush forage or high-moisture feed in the days before shipping, holding back grain and rich legumes for at least 12 hours, and stopping water only 2 to 3 hours before loading. The idea is not to starve the animal. Just don't stuff it with wet feed on the last day.

New feeder stock arriving at your farm also need rest, water and a few quiet days before you change their feed or handle them again.

## Setting up the truck

Hired six-wheeler or your own truck with racks, check these before loading:

- Floor: no holes, no missing planks, and something to stop slipping. The land transport order asks for bedding to absorb urine and dung and reduce slipping. Rice hull, sawdust, dayami or rubber mats are commonly used.
- Sides: high and strong enough that an animal cannot jump or fall out, with no nails, wire ends or sharp edges sticking out.
- Partitions: on a long truck, dividers stop the whole load from sliding to one end when the driver brakes. Keep horned animals, bulls and very small animals apart.
- Shade and airflow: a roof or tarp over the top, but leave the sides open enough for air to move.

On space, the clearest Philippine figure we found is in the sea transport order. It sets 9 head of cattle or carabao averaging about 450 kg in a standard 20-foot cattle van, which works out to roughly 1.65 square meters per head. Treat it as a guide for land trucks too. Too tight, and an animal that goes down gets trampled.

## Loading and unloading

Calm animals load faster than scared ones.

- Use a proper loading ramp or chute with solid sides.
- Work quietly. No shouting, no hitting, no twisting tails. Let the animals walk up at their own pace, a few at a time.
- On arrival, unload right away into a shaded pen with water.

## Timing and heat

The tag-init heat is hard on cattle in a truck. Travel in the early morning, late afternoon or at night, not at midday. On long trips, stop in the shade and check every animal. If one is down, get it up or move it to a separate section before it gets trampled.

## Shrink and your selling price

Shrink is the weight an animal loses while off feed and water and on the road. Most of it is dung and urine, which comes back once it eats and drinks again. A smaller part comes from the body itself and takes longer to regain.

Mississippi State University Extension gives these approximate figures for cattle without feed and water:

| Condition | Approximate weight lost |
|---|---|
| First 3 to 4 hours | about 1% of body weight per hour |
| The next 8 to 10 hours | about 0.25% per hour |
| 8 hours in a moving truck | about 5.5% |
| 16 hours in a moving truck | about 7.9% |

These are from studies abroad. Heat and rough handling make shrink worse, so expect similar or higher losses here.

What this means for you: if you sell by liveweight at the buyer's scale, every kilo lost on the road is a kilo you don't get paid for. Before you agree on a price, settle where and when the animal will be weighed, at your farm before loading or at the buyer's end. Buyers who weigh at your farm sometimes deduct a percentage for shrink, so ask how much. If you sell mata-mata, shrink still shows. A tired, hollow-looking animal gets lower offers.

## Moving cattle between islands

Cattle from Masbate, Mindoro and other islands come to Luzon by boat. DA Administrative Order No. 2 (2003) covers sea transport. Among its rules:

- Animals cannot be shipped without valid BAI documents, and a quarantine officer can refuse sick or disabled animals at the port.
- The shipper must send a caretaker with the animals and bring feed and water for the whole voyage, at least 20% more than the expected need.
- Large animals must be checked at least three times a day on board.

DA has issued supplemental guidelines since then, so talk to the shipping company and your provincial vet well ahead.

## Summary

- Get the health certificate, shipping permit and ownership papers ready days before the trip, and ask your MAO what your province requires.
- Load only fit animals, feed dry feed before shipping, and give water until a few hours before loading.
- Use a truck with a non-slip floor, strong sides, partitions and shade, and don't overload it.
- Load and unload calmly on a ramp. Electric prods and making cattle jump are not allowed.
- Travel in the cooler hours and keep trips under 12 hours of continuous travel.
- Agree on where the animal is weighed before you agree on the price, because shrink can take several percent off its weight.
