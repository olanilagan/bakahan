In fattening, weight is money. You buy by the kilo or by eye, you sell by liveweight or by eye, and in between you are paying for feeds every day. The real weight of your animals is what tells you if the feeds are paying for themselves.

## Why weight matters

When you buy feeder cattle, an honest weight keeps you from overpaying for a thin animal that only looks big. When you sell, it protects you from a viajero (livestock trader) who estimates low. In between, weighing gives you the daily gain. That is the only way to know if your ration is worth what you pay for it.

If you never weigh, you only find out at selling time whether the past months made money. By then it is too late to change the feeds.

## Using a livestock scale

A scale is still the surest way. Some livestock auction markets, cooperatives, LGUs and bigger farms have a cattle scale. Ask your Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) if there is one nearby that small raisers can use.

If you buy your own, the common setup is a platform with load bars under a strong pen or chute. A few reminders:

- Put the scale on level, firm ground and check it now and then with a known weight.
- Weigh one animal at a time unless you only need a group average.
- Let the animal stand still before you read the number. A moving animal makes the reading jump.

## Weight tapes and girth formulas

When there is no scale, most people measure the heart girth. That is the distance around the chest, just behind the front legs and shoulder blades. A UPLB study by Valdez (2003) found heart girth was the best single body measurement for predicting weight in cattle and several other animals, and that adding other measurements improved accuracy.

A weight tape is a measuring tape printed with estimated kilos instead of centimeters. You wrap it around the heart girth and read the weight straight off. It is quick and cheap, and good enough for tracking gain on the same animal.

You can also use a plain tape measure and a formula. Oregon State University Extension gives this one for cattle:

> heart girth x heart girth x body length ÷ 300 = weight in pounds (measurements in inches)

Body length is measured from the point of the shoulder to the pin bone. Converted to centimeters and kilos, the same formula is roughly:

> heart girth (cm) x heart girth (cm) x body length (cm) ÷ 10,840 = weight in kg

For example, a steer with a 150 cm heart girth and 130 cm body length works out to about 270 kg. Treat that as an estimate, not a weight you can sell by.

## How accurate are tapes and formulas?

They are estimates, and they can be far off on our kind of cattle. Many commercial tapes and formulas were made for European breeds. Those are built differently from Brahman, native and other zebu-type cattle, which have a hump, loose skin and a dewlap.

- A 2025 study in Brazil on Guzerá (a zebu breed) and Wagyu x Guzerá cattle found the weight tape consistently read heavier than a calibrated digital scale, and the error differed between the two groups.
- A Kenyan study built a girth model just for local zebu cattle. Even that model could be off by up to about 20%. The authors accepted that as good enough for dosing medicine, not for selling.

So use the tape to compare an animal against itself over time, measured the same way by the same person. If you want better numbers, weigh a few of your animals on a real scale, tape them on the same day, and note how far off the tape is. Then you know how much to adjust the tape reading for your own animals. When you buy or sell a whole batch, push for a scale weight.

## Body condition scoring

Body condition score (BCS) is judging fatness by eye and by hand, separate from weight. A tall, bony steer and a short, well-covered one can weigh the same but are very different animals to a buyer.

In the scale used by many extension services, cattle are scored from 1 (extremely thin) to 9 (extremely fat). You look and feel along the spine, ribs, hip and pin bones, tailhead and brisket. When ribs and backbone are easy to see, the animal is thin. When those areas feel smooth and padded, it is well covered. When buying feeders, a thin animal that is healthy and has a big frame often has room to gain. Score at the start and again near selling time so you can see the change.

## How often to weigh

Weigh or tape every animal when it arrives, so you have a starting point. After that, every two to four weeks is a practical interval for a small fattening operation. Weigh too often and you mostly see changes in what is in the belly. Weigh again just before selling.

Write it all down: date, weight, method and who measured. See our records guide for a simple layout. A notebook or a simple spreadsheet is enough to work out average daily gain.

## Same time of day, and shrink

The rumen (the big first stomach) holds a lot of feed and water, so its weight changes during the day. Weigh at the same time and in the same condition each time, ideally in the morning before feeding.

Weight lost from an empty belly or from transport is called shrink. Mississippi State University Extension says cattle without feed and water lose about 1% of their weight per hour for the first 3 to 4 hours, then more slowly, and shrink of feeder cattle commonly falls between 2 and 8% of liveweight. Most of that is manure and urine, which the animal gains back quickly once it eats and drinks again.

This matters when buying and selling. An animal weighed right after a long trip to market will weigh less than it did at home. A buyer may also ask you to hold back feed and water before weighing. Settle the terms with the buyer before loading, not after.

## Summary

- Regular weighing tells you if your feeds are paying for themselves.
- A scale is best. Ask your MAO or the nearest livestock market if one is available.
- Tapes and girth formulas are estimates and can read high on Brahman and other zebu-type cattle, so use them mainly to track the same animal over time.
- Check your tape against a scale on a few animals to learn how far off it is.
- Weigh at the same time of day, before feeding, and remember that shrink after a trip can be several percent.
- Use body condition score alongside weight to judge how well an animal is finishing.
