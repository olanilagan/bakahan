Most raisers sell their animals live and never think about what happens afterward. But if you sell to a butcher, a meat trader or a slaughterhouse, the rules on slaughter and inspection affect you. They also protect your buyers' trust in the meat that comes from your animals.

## Who does what

The National Meat Inspection Service (NMIS) is the national agency for meat safety. According to NMIS material, meat inspection in slaughterhouses is done by authorized NMIS or local government meat inspectors who completed the Basic Meat Inspection Course. Cities and municipalities are expected to set up and run public slaughterhouses by ordinance, following national standards.

Slaughterhouses need an NMIS license to operate. A 2022 NMIS circular sets the implementing rules for licensing. A December 2025 news report says NMIS planned new guidelines to make licensing easier for slaughterhouses and other meat establishments. Since the guidelines may have changed since then, check with NMIS or your local government for the current rules.

## What the inspection does

An official inspector assigned to a licensed establishment checks the animal and its meat. The NMIS annual report describes the Meat Inspection Certificate (MIC) as the certificate of wholesomeness of meat after inspection. In plain terms, this is the paper that says the meat passed.

Inspection usually covers the animal before slaughter and the carcass and organs after. The inspector looks for signs of disease that make meat unfit for sale. Ask the slaughterhouse office for the details, because procedures and fees are set locally.

## What it means for you

- Sell to licensed buyers. Meat from an animal slaughtered outside an inspected slaughterhouse has not been checked by anyone, and local rules on this differ. If a buyer wants to slaughter in the backyard, ask your MAO or municipal veterinarian what is allowed.
- Healthy animals only. A sick animal may be condemned at inspection, and you lose the sale. Do not send an animal that has been treated recently or that looks ill. Follow the withdrawal period on any medicine label and your vet's advice.
- Keep your own papers. Bring proof of ownership and any transport or health certificates required in your area. Large cattle are supposed to be registered under the anti-cattle rustling law. Ask your municipal treasurer or MAO how it works locally.
- Report suspected disease. If you see blisters in the mouth or on the feet with drooling and limping, do not move or sell any animal. Report to your municipal or provincial veterinarian or BAI. BAI has guidelines on the movement and slaughter of animals susceptible to foot-and-mouth disease.

## Before the animal goes

Good handling before slaughter affects meat quality and the weight you get.

- Rest the animal after transport, in the shade with water, if the buyer allows time.
- Avoid rough handling, beating and long waits in the sun. Stress and injury cause bruises, which are cut away and lost.
- Do not overload the truck. A downed animal is a loss for everyone.
- Remove feed some hours before slaughter if the slaughterhouse asks for it, but keep water available. Ask the slaughterhouse what they prefer.

## Liveweight and carcass pricing

Some buyers pay by liveweight, and some pay by the weight of the carcass after slaughter. Our business guide explains how dressing percentage works. If you sell by carcass weight, it helps to be at the slaughterhouse and watch the weighing. Ask the slaughterhouse staff how the weight is recorded and how fees are charged. Write down the numbers so you can compare with other sales.

## Selling meat yourself

Some raisers think of slaughtering and selling meat directly to earn more. This is a different business from selling live animals. You would need to use a licensed slaughterhouse and follow the rules for transport and sale of meat, and your local government may require permits, such as a business permit and sanitary requirements. Cold chain and market access also cost money. Talk to your MAO, the local meat inspector and the Department of Trade and Industry before you invest.

## Records worth keeping

Write down every sale: the date, the buyer, the live weight if you have it, the carcass weight if you were told one, the price per kilo and the total paid. If part of an animal was condemned, note which part and the reason given. After a few sales you will see which buyers pay fairly and which weigh generously in their own favor.

These notes also help when you decide what to raise next. If condemned livers keep coming up, for example, talk to your vet about worms and liver fluke in your herd, because that points to a problem on the farm and not just bad luck at the slaughterhouse.

## Questions to ask a buyer

- Where will the animal be slaughtered, and is that slaughterhouse licensed?
- How will it be priced, by liveweight or carcass weight?
- Who weighs, and can I watch?
- When do I get paid, and by what method?
- What happens if the inspector condemns the animal or part of it?

Write down the answers, and keep the receipt.

## Summary

- NMIS licenses slaughterhouses, and inspection is done by NMIS or LGU meat inspectors.
- The Meat Inspection Certificate shows that the meat was inspected and passed.
- Sell healthy animals, follow medicine withdrawal periods and handle animals gently before slaughter.
- Keep your ownership papers and ask your MAO what permits apply on your route.
- Check with NMIS or your local government for current licensing rules before you plan to sell meat yourself.
