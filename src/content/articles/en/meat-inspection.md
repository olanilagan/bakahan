Most raisers sell their animals live and never think about what happens afterward. But if you sell to a butcher, a meat trader or a slaughterhouse, the rules on slaughter and inspection affect you. They also protect your buyers' trust in the meat that comes from your animals.

## Who does what

The main law is the Meat Inspection Code (Republic Act 9296 of 2004), amended by Republic Act 10536 in 2013. It makes the National Meat Inspection Service (NMIS), under the DA, the national authority on meat inspection and meat hygiene. Local governments regulate the building and running of their own slaughterhouses and local meat inspection, following national standards.

Under Section 14 of the Code, only meat inspectors appointed or designated by NMIS or by the local government may inspect meat, and all of them must be trained by NMIS. NMIS runs a Basic Meat Inspection Course for LGU employees who will become meat inspectors, and it deputizes some LGU inspectors to do NMIS work.

Slaughterhouses and other meat establishments need a license to operate from NMIS. In December 2025, Philstar reported that NMIS was consulting on interim guidelines to make licensing faster, with shorter validity for new licenses. The rules may have changed since, so check with NMIS or your local government for the current ones.

## What the inspection does

The Code requires inspection at two points. Before slaughter, an inspector checks each animal that is meant for sale (ante-mortem inspection). After slaughter, the inspector checks the carcass and organs (post-mortem inspection). Carcasses that pass are marked "Inspected and Passed". Those that fail are marked "Inspected and Condemned" and destroyed as food.

NMIS describes the Meat Inspection Certificate (MIC) as proof that the meat was fit for human consumption on the date and time of inspection. It is issued by an NMIS meat inspector, or a deputized LGU inspector, assigned to an NMIS-licensed establishment. In plain terms, this is the paper that says the meat passed.

Fees and day-to-day procedures are set by each slaughterhouse and LGU, so ask the slaughterhouse office for the details.

## What it means for you

- Sell to buyers who use licensed slaughterhouses. Under RA 10536, meat from an unregistered or unaccredited establishment that skipped inspection is "hot meat", and selling or transporting it can bring a prison term of more than 6 and up to 12 years, a fine of ₱100,000 to ₱1,000,000, or both. If a buyer wants to slaughter in the backyard, ask your MAO or municipal veterinarian what your LGU allows.
- Healthy animals only. A sick animal may be condemned at inspection, and you lose the sale. Do not send an animal that has been treated recently or that looks ill. Follow the withdrawal period on any medicine label and your vet's advice.
- Keep your own papers. Under the Anti-Cattle Rustling Law (PD 533), large cattle must be registered with the city or municipal treasurer, who issues a certificate of ownership. Moving cattle within the province needs a permit from the treasurer, and moving them to another province needs more papers, including a certificate from the Provincial Veterinarian that the animals are free of disease. Ask your MAO what is required on your route.
- Report suspected disease. If you see blisters in the mouth or on the feet with drooling and limping, do not move or sell any animal. Report to your municipal or provincial veterinarian or BAI. A 2003 DA order on foot-and-mouth disease requires animals for slaughter to go straight from farm to slaughterhouse with a veterinary health certificate and shipping permit, and to be slaughtered within 24 hours of arrival. Ask your MAO which of these papers are still being asked for in your area.

## Before the animal goes

Good handling before slaughter affects meat quality and the weight you get.

- Rest the animal after transport, in the shade with water, if the buyer allows time.
- Avoid rough handling, beating and long waits in the sun. Stress and injury cause bruises, which are cut away and lost.
- Do not overload the truck. A downed animal is a loss for everyone.
- Remove feed some hours before slaughter if the slaughterhouse asks for it, but keep water available. Ask the slaughterhouse what they prefer.

## Liveweight and carcass pricing

Some buyers pay by liveweight, and some pay by the weight of the carcass after slaughter. Our business guide explains how dressing percentage works. If you sell by carcass weight, it helps to be at the slaughterhouse and watch the weighing. Ask the slaughterhouse staff how the weight is recorded and how fees are charged. Write down the numbers so you can compare with other sales.

## Selling meat yourself

Some raisers think of slaughtering and selling meat directly to earn more. This is a different business from selling live animals. You would need to use a licensed slaughterhouse and follow the rules for transport and sale of meat, and your local government may require permits, such as a business permit and sanitary requirements. Cold chain and market access also cost money. Talk to your MAO, the local meat inspector and the Department of Trade and Industry before you invest.

## Records worth keeping

Write down every sale: the date, the buyer, the live weight if you have it, the carcass weight if you were told one, the price per kilo and the total paid. If part of an animal was condemned, note which part and the reason given. After a few sales you will see which buyers pay fairly and which weigh in their own favor.

These notes also help when you decide what to raise next. If condemned livers keep coming up, for example, talk to your vet about worms and liver fluke in your herd, because that points to a problem on the farm and not just bad luck at the slaughterhouse.

## Questions to ask a buyer

- Where will the animal be slaughtered, and is that slaughterhouse licensed?
- How will it be priced, by liveweight or carcass weight?
- Who weighs, and can I watch?
- When do I get paid, and by what method?
- What happens if the inspector condemns the animal or part of it?

Write down the answers, and keep the receipt.

## Summary

- NMIS licenses slaughterhouses, and meat is inspected by NMIS-trained inspectors from NMIS or the LGU.
- Animals are checked before slaughter and carcasses after. The Meat Inspection Certificate shows the meat passed.
- Meat from uninspected slaughter is "hot meat", and selling it carries heavy penalties.
- Sell healthy animals, follow medicine withdrawal periods and handle animals gently before slaughter.
- Keep your certificate of ownership and ask your MAO what permits apply on your route.
