If you keep cattle in a kural (pen) and cut-and-carry their feed, your grass plot is the most important part of the farm. A good stand of Napier can be cut again and again for years, but only if you plant it right, feed the soil and cut at the right time. Here are the grasses and legumes raisers here actually plant, and how to look after them.

## Choosing grasses

Napier (*Pennisetum purpureum*) is the usual choice for cut-and-carry. It grows tall, yields heavily and regrows after every cut. Its deep roots make it hardy in drought, according to the Tropical Forages fact sheet, though it still needs moisture to keep producing. It needs full sun and well-drained soil, and it does not survive flooding.

Pakchong 1, sold as "Super Napier", is a Napier and pearl millet cross from Thailand. Its developer told a PCC seminar that it reaches about 16 to 18% crude protein and can be cut every 45 to 48 days. Those numbers come from the developer, so treat them as a best case.

Other grasses used in the Philippines, as listed by PCC:

- Guinea grass (*Panicum maximum*, including the improved Mombasa variety now grown at PCC stations). Good for cut-and-carry and grazing on fertile, well-drained soil. It does not like waterlogging and most varieties don't handle a dry season longer than four to five months.
- Signal grass (*Brachiaria decumbens*) and related Brachiarias like Mulato II. Signal grass takes heavy grazing and tolerates acid, poor soils. Better for a grazing paddock than for cutting. Tropical Forages warns that in some areas young cattle eating young signal grass can get a skin and liver problem (photosensitization). Ask your Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) if this has been seen locally.
- Para grass, which PCC also lists as a good silage grass.

## Adding legumes

Grass alone is low in protein once it gets old. PCC plants legumes and shrubs alongside its grasses: ipil-ipil, kakawate, rensonii, flemingia, centrosema, stylo and *Arachis pintoi* (mani-manihan). PROSEA notes that centrosema grows well with Napier and that ipil-ipil can be planted in rows between Napier. An easy start is a living fence of kakawate or a row of ipil-ipil along the edge of the plot. See our feeds guide for how much legume to feed.

## Preparing the land

Napier wants deep soil of at least moderate fertility. Plow and harrow as you would for corn, and pull out the old roots of cogon and other weeds. If the field holds water after rain, plant on raised beds or ridges.

Have the soil tested before you buy fertilizer. Your MAO can tell you where to bring a soil sample. Tropical Forages says guinea grass needs lime if the soil pH is below 5.

## Planting Napier from cuttings

Napier is planted from stem pieces, not seed.

- Take cuttings from the lower two-thirds of mature stems. Each piece should have at least three nodes (the joints on the stem).
- Push each cutting into moist soil at about a 45 degree angle, with two nodes under the ground and one above.
- You can also lay cuttings flat in a furrow 5 to 10 cm deep and cover them.
- Plant at the start of the rainy season so the roots settle before tag-init.

For spacing, Tropical Forages gives rows 0.5 to 2 m apart and plants 0.3 to 1 m apart within the row. Plant closer where rain is plentiful and wider where it is dry. Your local agriculturist can tell you what works in your area.

Guinea grass can be planted from rooted pieces of the clump or from seed. Signal grass is usually sown from seed; PCC used 6 to 8 kg per hectare.

## Fertilizer and manure

A Napier plot that is cut often pulls a lot of nutrients out of the soil. Without fertilizer, PROSEA puts yields at about 2 to 10 tons of dry matter per hectare per year. Well-fertilized stands can give 6 to 40 tons. That big gap comes from feeding the soil.

- Use the manure from your kural. PCC spreads animal manure on its pastures. Spread it between rows after a cut.
- Add nitrogen after cutting, when the grass starts to regrow. Tropical Forages suggests about 150 to 300 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year for established Napier, split across the year. Base the actual amounts on your soil test.
- Don't dump a lot of manure or urea on at the end of a long drought. Feedipedia reports cases of nitrate poisoning in cattle when grass regrew fast after heavy fertilizing at the end of the dry season.

## Cutting height and interval

| Grass | First cut | Cut again every | Leave stubble of |
|---|---|---|---|
| Napier | about 3 to 4 months after planting (Feedipedia) | about 45 to 55 days (PCC) | about 15 to 30 cm (Tropical Forages) |
| Pakchong 1 | after it is well established | about 45 to 48 days (developer's claim) | same as Napier |
| Guinea grass (tall types) | when well established | about 4 weeks (Tropical Forages) | not below about 30 cm |

All figures are approximate and change with rain and soil. Two rules matter more than the exact number of days:

- Don't let Napier grow past about 1.5 m. Older grass is mostly stem. Feedipedia cites a study where protein fell from about 21% at 30 days of regrowth to under 4% at 70 days.
- Don't cut too low. Tropical Forages says cutting Napier at 10 cm or lower again and again weakens the stand until plants die.

## Ratooning and keeping the stand

After each cut, Napier regrows from the base (the ratoon). Weed between rows, add manure or fertilizer, and the same clumps keep producing. A stand weakens when the soil is not fed or the grass is cut too low. When clumps thin out, fill the gaps with fresh cuttings rather than replanting the whole field.

## How much area you need

There is no single figure for how much Napier one head needs, because yield depends on your soil, rain and fertilizer. As a guide, PCC estimates a 500-kg animal eats about 50 kg of fresh grass a day. For dry season silage, PCC figures about half a hectare of corn per animal for six months. Bring these numbers to your MAO or local agriculturist and plan your plot together.

## Managing in the dry season

- Cut extra grass in the rainy season and make silage from it. See our silage guide.
- Irrigate if you have water. Even a little water keeps Napier growing.
- Keep dayami (rice straw) and other crop residues on hand as backup.
- If you graze, signal grass stays green into the dry season better than its cousin *Brachiaria brizantha*, though it also slows down a lot.

## Summary

- Napier is the main cut-and-carry grass. Add guinea grass, signal grass and legumes like ipil-ipil and kakawate to suit your land.
- Plant Napier from three-node cuttings, two nodes buried, at the start of the rains.
- Feed the soil with kural manure and fertilizer based on a soil test.
- Cut Napier at about 45 to 55 days, before it passes 1.5 m, and leave 15 to 30 cm of stubble.
- Plan your plot size with your MAO, and make silage from rainy-season extra for tag-init.
