Ask a backyard raiser how much the last batch earned and you'll often get a guess. Without records you can't tell if the feeds were worth it, which animal grew slowest, or what your real cost was. A notebook and a pen, kept up every week, is enough to fix that.

## Give every animal an ID

Records only work if you can tell your animals apart on paper. The easiest way is an ear tag with a number. Write the same number on every page that talks about that animal. If you name your cattle, write the name beside the number, but always use the number.

Ear tags and papers also matter outside the farm.

- Under PD 533, the Anti-Cattle Rustling Law, large cattle should be registered with the city or municipal treasurer before they reach six months old, and a certificate of ownership is issued.
- To move cattle from one province to another, PD 533 requires a permit and the certificate of ownership plus a veterinary certification. Within a province, a permit from the municipal or city treasurer of the place of origin is enough under the decree.
- Local offices also ask for papers. In Manolo Fortich, Bukidnon, for example, a Veterinary Health Certificate for cattle requires a Certificate of Ownership of Large Animal from the Municipal Treasurer's Office.
- The BAI's local shipping permit form asks how the animals are identified, and an ear tag is one of the options.

Rules and fees differ by town and province, so ask your Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) and municipal treasurer what they need before you buy or ship.

## What to write down

Keep it to things you'll actually use.

- Purchase. Date bought, where (for example, the auction at Padre Garcia or a viajero, the travelling cattle trader), seller's name, price, weight on arrival, and what papers came with the animal.
- Weights. Weigh on arrival, then every two to four weeks, always at the same time of day (before feeding is best). If you have no scale, a weight tape is better than guessing, but write down that it was a tape estimate.
- Feeds. What you bought, how many kilos or sacks, and how much you paid. For your own Napier or dayami, note roughly how many bundles a day. If a group of animals shares one feed trough, record feed for the whole kural (pen).
- Health. Date, which animal, what you saw (cough, diarrhea, not eating), what was given and who gave it. Write down the product name and the vet's instructions, and follow the vet on doses and withdrawal periods.
- Other costs. Ear tags, transport, labor you paid, permits, electricity or water if it's separate.
- Sales. Date, buyer, sale weight, price per kilo or per head, and any deductions or commissions.
- Deaths and losses. Date, which animal, and the likely cause. If the animal is insured, take photos showing the ear tag right away.

## A simple layout

You don't need software. A thick notebook works. Divide it into sections.

1. Animal list. One line per animal: ear tag number, sex, color and markings, approximate age, date in, purchase price, arrival weight, and later the date out and how it left (sold, died).
2. Weight page. Columns for date, then one column per ear tag number. Each weighing day is one row.
3. Feed and expense page. Date, item, quantity, amount in pesos. Add up each month.
4. Health page. Date, ear tag number, problem, treatment, who treated, cost.
5. Sales page. Date, ear tag number, buyer, weight, price, net amount received.

If you're comfortable with a phone or computer, the same pages work as tabs in Google Sheets or Excel, and the spreadsheet does the adding for you. But a notebook that is kept up beats a spreadsheet nobody updates.

## Computing average daily gain

Average daily gain (ADG) tells you how fast an animal is putting on weight. Nebraska Extension gives the formula as:

ADG = (ending weight minus starting weight) ÷ number of days

Here is an example for one animal. The numbers are made up and rounded to show the math. They are not targets or current prices.

| Item | Example |
|---|---|
| Weight on arrival | 180 kg |
| Weight after 150 days | 290 kg |
| Kilos gained | 110 kg |
| ADG (110 ÷ 150) | about 0.73 kg per day |
| Feeds and forage bought | ₱16,500 |
| Vitamins, deworming, vet | ₱1,500 |
| Transport, tags, permits, paid labor | ₱2,000 |
| Total cost of gain (not counting purchase price) | ₱20,000 |
| Cost per kilo gained (20,000 ÷ 110) | about ₱182 |

Work out ADG at every weighing, not only at the end. If one animal is clearly behind the others on the same feeds, have it checked for worms or illness, or think about selling it early.

## Computing cost per kilo gained

Cost per kilo gained is what you spend to put one more kilo on the animal. Nebraska Extension counts feed, vet, interest, labor and death loss in this cost, then divides by the kilos gained.

Cost per kilo gained = total fattening costs ÷ kilos gained

In the example above, that is about ₱182 for every kilo added. Compare it with the liveweight price you expect per kilo when you sell. If each new kilo costs you more than a buyer will pay for it, keeping the animal longer loses money. That is usually your sign to sell. ## How records help with loans, insurance and selling

Loans. Lenders want to see that the project can pay back. ACPC's Agri-Negosyo program, for example, leaves the document requirements to the lending conduit, so ask the bank or cooperative what they need. A record of your past batches (what you bought, what you spent, what you sold for) convinces a lender far more than a plan on paper.

Insurance. PCIC's livestock mortality insurance application asks for each animal's breed, age, color and ear mark or tag number, and its citizen's charter lists a Certificate of Ownership of Large Animal for cattle. For a death claim, PCIC asks for photos of the dead animal showing its identifying marks such as the ear tag or brand. No tag and no records make a claim much harder. Check the current requirements with your PCIC office or MAO, because they change.

Selling. A buyer who can see an animal's weight history, deworming and vaccination dates, and clean ownership papers knows what they are getting. It also helps you set your price, because you know the animal's weight and what it cost you.

## Summary

- Ear tag every animal and use the tag number on every page.
- Record purchase, weights, feeds, health treatments, other costs, sales and losses.
- A notebook with five sections is enough. A spreadsheet is optional.
- ADG = kilos gained ÷ days. Cost per kilo gained = fattening costs ÷ kilos gained.
- Keep ownership and vet papers in order. Lenders, PCIC and buyers will ask for them.
- Ask your MAO and municipal treasurer about registration and transport permits in your area.
