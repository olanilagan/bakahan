Most cows give birth on their own without any help. Your job is to know what normal looks like, so you can tell early when something is wrong and call the vet in time. After that, the first few hours of the calf's life decide a lot about how healthy it will be.

## Signs that calving is near

As the due date gets close, check the cow at least morning and evening. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Extension lists these signs that calving is coming:

- A thick, clear string of mucus hanging from the vulva.
- She eats less.
- She moves away from the herd to be alone.

Move her to a clean, dry, shaded spot. Have the number of your vet or the Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) technician ready before the day comes.

## Normal calving and when to call for help

The real labour starts when the water bag shows at the vulva. According to Wisconsin Extension, the calf should come out within 2 to 5 hours from that point, and many cows deliver much faster. In a normal birth you see two front feet, soles down, with the nose resting on top of them.

Call your veterinarian if:

- The cow has been straining for about an hour with no progress.
- She is straining hard and nothing is showing.
- Only one leg shows, or the head is turned back, or you see the tail or hind feet first (soles up).
- The cow is exhausted and has stopped trying.
- The afterbirth has not come out about 12 hours after calving. Don't pull it out. Call the vet, especially if she has a fever or stops eating.

Don't pull the calf yourself with ropes or a vehicle. That can kill the calf and badly injure the cow. Calling for help early is better than waiting too long.

## The first hour

Once the calf is out:

- Make sure it is breathing. AHDB, the UK farm advisory body, says to clear mucus from the mouth and nostrils, and tickle inside the nose with a clean straw to make it snort.
- Let the cow lick the calf dry.
- A calf sitting up on its chest within about 20 minutes is a good sign. AHDB advises getting a vet's advice if it is still panting after 20 minutes.

## Colostrum comes first

Colostrum (the thick, yellowish first milk) carries the antibodies that protect the calf in its first weeks. The calf's gut can only absorb these antibodies well for a short time after birth, and that ability drops fast within the first day.

- AHDB says the calf should drink colostrum within six hours of birth, and the earlier the better. It suggests about 3 litres, or about 10% of the calf's body weight, then another similar feed later.
- Watch the calf actually suckle. A calf lying quietly right after birth has probably not fed yet.
- Feel its belly. A calf that has drunk well has a full stomach.
- A cow with a full, tight udder hours after calving may mean the calf is not drinking.
- If the calf is too weak to suck, or the cow won't let it, call your vet or technician. They can help you milk the cow and feed the colostrum, or tube-feed it safely. Don't force milk into a weak calf's mouth, because it can go into the lungs.

## Navel care

The wet navel cord is an open door for germs. AHDB recommends dipping the navel in iodine tincture right after birth and again a few hours later, especially if the cow keeps licking it. Ask your vet or the agri-vet store which product to use. Watch the navel over the next days. If it becomes swollen, hot, smelly or leaks pus, call the vet.

## Keeping the calf dry and protected

Newborn calves get sick more easily in wet, dirty places, and this matters most during the rainy season.

- Give the cow and calf a dry, clean pen or shed with a roof, good airflow and no strong draught.
- Clean out wet bedding and dung often. Fresh *dayami* (rice straw) makes good bedding.
- Keep them in the shade on hot days. Young calves are among the first to suffer from heat stress.
- Keep dogs and other animals away from the newborn.

## Scours (diarrhea)

Scours is one of the most common problems in young calves. Oregon State University Extension says bacterial scours usually hits calves under two weeks old, and viral scours mostly at one to three weeks. The danger is the water and salts the calf loses. A calf with scours can die within a couple of days if it is not treated in time.

Watch for:

- Watery dung and a dirty tail.
- Sunken eyes.
- Skin on the neck that stays tented for more than two seconds after you pinch it.
- Weakness, and a calf that stops suckling.

What to do:

- Keep the calf drinking its mother's milk. Oregon State notes milk is still its main source of energy.
- Ask your vet about oral electrolytes. Don't give antibiotics on your own.
- Call the vet right away if the calf can't stand, won't suck, or there is blood in the dung. A calf that is down usually needs fluids by drip, which only a vet can give.
- Separate the sick calf and wash your hands and boots after handling it.

## Pneumonia

Farmers Weekly lists these signs of pneumonia in calves:

- Fever (a rectal temperature above 39.5°C).
- Discharge from the nose or eyes.
- Coughing and fast or hard breathing.
- Slow feeding.
- Drooping ears.

NDSU Extension also warns about pneumonia in suckling calves after a stretch of hot days. Look for calves standing apart from the herd and mothers with full udders. Pneumonia needs a vet's diagnosis and treatment. Call early, because calves go downhill fast.

## Identification and records

Tag or mark each calf soon after birth, and write down its birth date, its mother and, if you can, its weight. DA Administrative Order No. 14 (1994) on the cattle breeding program asks breeders to keep records of birth and weaning weights. The DA is also rolling out new livestock data systems by 2027, so ask your MAO whether ear tags or registration are needed for the programs you want to join. Your vet or technician can show you how to put on an ear tag properly.

## Castration

If you plan to raise bull calves for fattening, ask your veterinarian whether to castrate, when, and which method to use. Do not do it yourself without training.

## Weaning

DA Administrative Order No. 14 says beef calves stay with their mothers until weaning at about 8 to 10 months old, and that creep feeding (extra feed only the calves can reach) may be used. Some raisers wean earlier. Ask your MAO or a PCC or BAI technician what fits your feeds and your cows' condition.

## Summary

- Know the normal signs of calving, and call the vet if labour stalls for about an hour or the calf is in the wrong position.
- Make sure the calf drinks colostrum within the first few hours, and no later than six.
- Dip the navel and keep the calf in a dry, clean pen.
- Scours and pneumonia can kill calves fast. Call the vet early, especially for a calf that is down or not suckling.
- Tag and record each calf, ask your vet about castration, and plan weaning at around 8 to 10 months as the DA guideline says.
