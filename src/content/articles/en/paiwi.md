Many people in the barangay have the time and the grass to raise a cow but not the money to buy one. Others have the money but no time or land. The paiwi system (also called *paalaga* or *alagaan, hatian*) brings the two together, and it has been done in Filipino farm towns for generations. It works well when both sides agree clearly from the start, and it goes sour fast when they don't.

## How paiwi works

The idea is simple. The owner provides the animal. The caretaker, sometimes called the *tagapag-alaga*, feeds it, houses it in his kural and looks after it every day. When the animal gives birth or is sold, the two share what it produced.

The Tagalog root word is *iwi*. Wiktionary defines it as taking care of a domestic animal on behalf of the owner, "while possibly getting half of its offspring as his share." So the half-and-half idea is old and well known, but the actual terms are whatever the two people agree on.

DAR Secretary Conrado Estrella III described it in a 2023 BusinessMirror report the same way: a livestock owner entrusts animals to a caretaker, and the caretaker shares in the income when the animals are sold.

## Common ways to share

Terms differ by province, by family and by the kind of animal. These are the setups you are most likely to hear about. Treat the splits below as examples to discuss, not as a standard.

- Breeding cow, sharing calves. The owner gives a heifer or cow. Calves are divided, often alternating (one for the caretaker, the next for the owner) or by agreed turns. The mother cow stays the owner's.
- Fattener, sharing the gain. The owner buys a feeder animal. When it is sold, the owner first takes back what the animal cost. What is left over (the gain) is divided by an agreed percentage.
- Owner shoulders more costs. If the owner also pays for feeds, vitamins and the vet, the caretaker's share is usually smaller, because he is giving only labor and forage.

Here is an example of a gain-sharing computation. These are made-up round numbers to show the math, not current prices.

| Item | Example amount |
|---|---|
| Owner buys feeder animal | ₱50,000 |
| Animal sold after fattening | ₱80,000 |
| Gain (sale minus purchase) | ₱30,000 |
| Caretaker's share at 50% | ₱15,000 |
| Owner gets back | ₱50,000 + ₱15,000 = ₱65,000 |

Notice that the caretaker only gets paid at the end. That's months of cutting Napier and hauling water with nothing in hand, so some owners give a small allowance or agree that the caretaker keeps any manure he can sell. Put those side deals in writing too.

## What to agree on in writing

Most paiwi deals in the province are verbal, and that's where the trouble starts. A one-page written agreement, signed by both and by a witness (a barangay kagawad is a good choice), saves a lot of heartache. It should answer these questions:

- Which animal. Describe it: sex, color, markings, approximate age, ear tag number if any, and its weight or price at turnover. Take a photo together with the caretaker.
- How long. For a fattener, until sale or a fixed number of months. For a breeder, how many calvings or years.
- Who pays for what. Feeds and concentrates, vitamins, deworming, vaccines, vet visits, breeding (bull service or artificial insemination), and transport to market.
- How the share is computed. Gain after purchase cost, or calves by turns. Say who gets the first calf.
- Who decides when to sell, and whether the caretaker can sell without the owner present. The safest answer is no.
- If the animal gets sick. The caretaker must report right away, and who calls the vet.
- If the animal dies. Death from disease or accident is usually a shared loss. Death from neglect (no feed, no water, no reporting) is usually the caretaker's responsibility. Spell it out.
- If the animal is stolen or goes missing. Who reports to the barangay and police, and whether the caretaker owes anything.
- If either side wants out early. How the animal is valued and returned.
- Registration. Under PD 533, the Anti-Cattle Rustling Law, large cattle are supposed to be registered with the municipal or city treasurer before they reach six months old, and a certificate of ownership is issued. The papers should stay in the owner's name. Ask your municipal treasurer or MAO how it is done locally.

## Risks for the owner

The biggest risk is losing the animal. A cattle raiser who wrote about running paiwi for eight years described a caretaker who sold a cow without permission and disappeared with the money. He only found out three days later. His advice was practical:

- Know the person well before you hand over an animal.
- Mark the animal (ear tag or brand) so it can't be sold quickly without questions.
- Visit regularly, or send someone you trust.

Poor care is the other risk. A thin animal at the end of six months means little or no gain for anyone. Agree on a basic feeding plan at the start and check weight from time to time.

## Risks for the caretaker

The caretaker carries the daily work and often the feed cost, but owns nothing until the split. If the animal dies, he may get nothing for months of labor. If the deal was verbal, the owner may change the terms at selling time, or pull the animal out early when it already looks good.

So a written agreement protects the caretaker just as much. He should also keep his own simple records of weights, vet visits and anything he spent.

## Government and cooperative programs with the same idea

The pass-on model that government uses is basically paiwi on a bigger scale.

- DA-PCC carabao programs. RA 7307 tells the Philippine Carabao Center to run a national dispersal program for smallholders and to work through farmers' cooperatives. In one PIA-reported case in Bataan, members who received pregnant dairy buffaloes had to pass on a female offspring, about 18 months old, to their cooperative so another member could get one.
- DA-SAAD cattle dispersal. In Zamboanga Peninsula in 2026, cattle were given to farmers' associations, and the offspring are meant to go to other members over time.
- Private paiwi in Masbate. In 2023 the Cattle Raisers Association of Masbate signed an agreement with DAR to supply cattle under a paiwi system to agrarian reform beneficiaries.

Each program has its own rules on who qualifies and what must be returned. Ask your MAO, the nearest PCC center or your provincial agrarian reform office what is open in your area.

## Summary

- Paiwi pairs an owner who has the animal with a caretaker who has the time, feed and space.
- Shares are usually by calves or by the gain after the purchase cost, but terms vary, so agree on them clearly.
- Put it in writing: the animal, how long, who pays which costs, how the share is computed, and what happens if it gets sick, dies or is stolen.
- Ear tag or mark the animal, keep the ownership papers in order, and visit regularly.
- Government dispersal programs from PCC and DA work on the same pass-on idea.
