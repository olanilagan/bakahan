A fattening animal only gains weight when it is comfortable. In our climate that means shade from the midday sun, a dry place to stand during the rainy season, and enough air moving through the shed. You do not need an expensive building, but moving a shed later costs more than building it right the first time.

## Choosing the site

Walk the land after a heavy rain before you decide. Where water stands for days, cattle will stand in mud, and mud brings foot problems and flies. The Philippine Agricultural Engineering Standard for cattle feedlots (PAES 405) asks for a site that is well drained and has free air circulation. Slightly higher ground is better than the low corner of the lot near the creek.

A few more things to check:

- **Wind and smell.** PAES 405 says the site should be where the prevailing winds will not carry odors to the farmhouse. Think about your neighbours' houses too. Smell and flies from a kural (cattle pen) can end up as a complaint at the barangay.
- **Water sources.** Keep the shed and the manure pile downhill and well away from your well, the deep well pump and any creek. Our sources did not give one national distance, so ask your Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) or municipal environment office what applies in your town.
- **Access.** You will be bringing in feeds and hauling out cattle. A shed that a truck or kuliglig can reach saves a lot of carrying, and PAES 405 also suggests putting the pen for market-ready animals near the service road.
- **Land use.** The location should follow the town's land use plan, so check with the MAO about permits before building anything big.

## Orientation, roof and airflow

PAES 405 recommends building the shed along an east-west axis. With the long side facing north and south, the sun crosses over the roof instead of shining deep into the pen in the morning and afternoon.

Heat collects under a low roof, especially GI sheets. The standard sets the top of the roof beam at least 2.5 meters from the floor. Many backyard sheds are lower, and you feel it at two in the afternoon.

Keep the sides open, or at least mostly open. The DA describes the usual backyard shelter as open-sided and located near the house or under shade trees. If wind-driven rain is a problem on one side, a removable sack or sawali panel on that side works better than a solid wall.

The roof should be steep enough for rain to run off quickly. PAES 405 gives a minimum slope of 25% for ordinary roofing and a much steeper slope for native materials like nipa and cogon, since these leak if the pitch is too flat.

## Floors

There are two common choices.

**Concrete** is easier to clean and lasts. PAES 405 says it should be skid resistant (rough finish, never smooth) and sloped about 2 to 4% toward a drain so urine and wash water run off. A slippery floor is how cattle fall and get hurt.

**Compacted soil** costs less and is easier on the feet, but it turns to mud in the rainy season unless the site drains well. The standard suggests a steeper slope for earth lots, about 4 to 7%. Many raisers use soil in the loafing area and put concrete only under the shed and in front of the troughs, where the animals stand the most. PAES 405 also recommends a concrete or gravel-packed apron in front of feed and water troughs on earth floors.

## Space per head

Philippine sources give different numbers depending on the setup. Use these as rough guides and adjust to the size of your animals.

| Area | Space per head | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Sheltered feeding area (backyard) | about 1.5 to 2 m² | DA, as reported by Edge Davao |
| Shed area (feedlot) | about 4 m² | PAES 405 |
| Fenced loafing or exercise area | about 5 m² | DA and PAES 405 |

The small figure suits tethered cattle brought in mostly to eat and sleep. Cattle penned all day need closer to the feedlot figures. Crowded pens get dirty fast, and the timid animals end up eating last.

## Feed troughs and water

PAES 405 gives about 750 mm (75 cm) of trough length per animal, so that each head can eat at the same time. It describes a trough about 40 cm deep, placed in front of the pen, with a horizontal rail about 1.0 to 1.2 meters above the pen floor so the cattle reach in without climbing into the feed.

Clean drinking water should be there all the time, not only at feeding. Concrete or GI troughs are easiest to scrub, and a float valve keeps the level up. Leftover feed and algae build up fast in the heat, so clean the water trough often.

## Manure and cleaning

Scrape or sweep out manure every day, and wash concrete floors when water allows. A wet, dirty pen is where flies breed.

Pile manure away from the shed and from water, under a cover so the rain does not wash it into the creek. Composted, it is good fertilizer for your Napier. Dig a simple drainage canal along the low side of the shed so wash water goes somewhere you planned, not into the neighbour's lot.

## Low-cost local materials

You can start small with what is around you.

- **Bamboo** is cheap and easy to work with for posts, rails and feed troughs, but it rots where it touches wet ground. Raise it on stones or concrete footings if you can.
- **Coco lumber** is sturdier than bamboo for posts and rafters and is easy to find in coconut areas. Ask the seller for mature, well-dried lumber.
- **Nipa or cogon** roofing is cooler under the sun than metal, but it needs replacing more often and must be tied down well.
- **GI sheets** last longer and are easier to repair after a storm, but they get very hot and noisy. Set them higher, and plant shade trees near the shed if you have space.

A DA-SAAD farmers' association in Bagulin, La Union started with a shelter of bamboo, tie wire and nails, then replaced it with a steel-framed one paid for by their cattle income.

Whatever you use, brace the posts and tie the roof down well. Typhoon preparation has its own guide.

## Fencing

The fence should be strong enough that cattle cannot push through when they are startled. PAES 405 suggests pen fences about 1.2 to 1.5 meters high, preferably of GI pipe, with posts set in concrete. In a backyard, bamboo or coco lumber rails on firm posts work for calm animals. Check every week for nails, broken wire and sharp edges, and do not use paint that may be toxic where cattle can lick it.

## Summary

- Build on high, well-drained ground, downwind and away from houses, wells and creeks.
- Run the shed east-west with open sides and a high roof (PAES 405 says at least 2.5 m to the top of the beam).
- Use a rough, sloped concrete floor where cattle stand most, and compacted soil only where water drains away.
- Plan roughly 1.5 to 4 m² of shelter per head plus about 5 m² of outdoor space, and about 75 cm of trough per head.
- Clean manure daily and keep it covered and away from water.
- Start with bamboo, coco lumber or nipa if money is tight, and ask your MAO about local rules before you build bigger.
