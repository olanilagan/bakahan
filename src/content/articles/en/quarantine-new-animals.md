Many herds are infected by a single animal from the trading post that looked fine on the day it was bought. Animals can carry disease before they show signs, and the trip home is stressful, which lowers resistance. A separate pen for new arrivals costs very little compared with losing a herd.

## Why isolation works

An animal bought at a market has met cattle from many farms and many places. It may be carrying worms, ticks, skin disease or germs from the trip. Some diseases take days to appear. If you put the animal straight into your herd, whatever it carries reaches everybody.

Extension sources abroad agree on a simple idea: keep the new animal separate long enough for illness to show. A Farms.com summary says new animals are typically held at least 30 days, and more often 60. Idaho guidance recommends a minimum of 30 days in a separate pen with no nose-to-nose contact over the fence. Some sources accept 21 to 30 days. These are foreign recommendations, so ask your municipal veterinarian what period suits the diseases in your area.

## Set up a simple quarantine pen

You do not need a clinic. What you need is distance and a separate set of tools.

- Place the pen away from your main kural, ideally downhill or downwind, so that water and air do not carry germs toward the herd.
- Use a solid fence or a wide gap. Idaho's guidance warns against fence-line contact. In one case described by an agriculture magazine, a fence that allowed nose-to-nose contact did not count as isolation.
- Give it shade, a clean trough and feed, as you would any animal. A stressed animal in a bare, hot pen recovers slowly.
- Keep the buckets, shovel, rope and halter for the quarantine pen separate. If you must share them, do the quarantine pen last and wash them after.
- Do not let manure from the quarantine pen wash toward your pasture or your cut-and-carry plot. Compost it separately, or ask the MAO about handling.

## Care for the new arrival

First, let it rest. Give clean water, and then feed in small amounts for the first day or two. Do not push it to eat.

Check it every day. The Wisconsin guide suggests taking the animal's temperature daily or at least every other day during quarantine. Ask your vet to show you how, and what a normal reading is. If you do not have a thermometer, at least watch for these signs:

- Not eating, or eating much less than expected.
- Cough, nasal discharge or heavy breathing.
- Loose, watery or bloody manure.
- Lameness, swollen joints or sores around the mouth and feet.
- Lumps or scabs on the skin, and many ticks.
- Standing apart from others with its head down.

If you see blisters in the mouth or on the feet with drooling or limping, do not move any animal. Call your municipal or provincial veterinarian or BAI right away. Do not wait out the quarantine period before reporting.

## Talk to your vet

Quarantine is the best time to ask your veterinarian about vaccination and deworming. A fecal exam can show which parasites the animal carries, so you treat only what is there. Do not give medicine by yourself and never guess a dose. Follow your vet's instructions and the product label, including any withdrawal period before slaughter.

Wisconsin's guide also suggests testing new animals for certain diseases during quarantine, but which diseases matter depends on the country. Ask your vet which tests make sense for the Philippines and your area.

## Dealing with ticks

New animals often bring ticks. Research from BAI found that tick fever, babesiosis and anaplasmosis, occurs across the Philippines, and improved breeds can be more susceptible than local cattle. Check the ears, neck, tail base and between the legs. Ask your vet what to use. Do not release the animal into the herd while it is carrying ticks.

## Visitors and vehicles

Quarantine is only one part of keeping disease out. Other ways in include:

- Trucks and vehicles that have carried other cattle. Ask for the vehicle to be washed before it loads your animals.
- Visitors, traders and neighbors who walk into your kural. Ask them to stay at the gate, or to wash and wear clean boots. Many diseases ride on boots and tires.
- Borrowed equipment, such as bulls, ropes or tools.
- Animals returning from a show, a paiwi arrangement or a market.

Treat returning animals as new ones. Put them in quarantine too.

## What to do at the end of the period

If the animal has stayed healthy for the full period, it can join the herd. Introduce it gradually, because cattle fight to establish their order. Do it in a large space with plenty of feed and water, and watch for the first few hours.

If it gets sick during quarantine, the clock starts again once it recovers, and ask your vet what to do next. Keep records of the date, the source and anything you saw. Those notes help if the animal turns out to be a problem.

## Summary

- Keep every newly bought animal in a separate pen before it joins the herd.
- Foreign extension sources recommend at least 30 days, often longer. Ask your municipal veterinarian for the right period locally.
- Put the pen away from the herd, with no nose-to-nose contact, and use separate tools.
- Watch the animal every day for poor appetite, cough, loose manure, lameness and ticks.
- Report mouth or foot blisters at once, and give no medicine without a vet.
