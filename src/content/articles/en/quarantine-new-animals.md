A whole herd can be infected by a single animal from the trading post that looked fine on the day it was bought. Animals can carry disease before they show signs, and the trip home is stressful, which lowers resistance. A separate pen for new arrivals costs very little compared with losing a herd.

## Why isolation works

An animal bought at a market has met cattle from many farms and many places. It may be carrying worms, ticks, skin disease or germs from the trip. Some diseases take days to appear. If you put the animal straight into your herd, whatever it carries reaches everybody.

The idea is simple: keep the new animal separate long enough for illness to show. The DA cattle guides from Region 3 and Caraga say not to mix new arrivals with the animals you already have. In 2024, the head of the DA Cordillera regulatory division advised animal raisers to isolate newcomers for 28 days, as reported by the Philippine News Agency. Sources abroad give similar or longer periods. The Wisconsin Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory says 30 days, and an Ohio State University extension article says at least 30 days and more typically 60. There is no single national rule for cattle that we could find, so ask your municipal veterinarian what period suits the diseases in your area.

## Set up a simple quarantine pen

You do not need a clinic. What you need is distance and a separate set of tools.

- Place the pen some distance from your main kural and downwind of it, as the Ohio State article suggests. Downhill is better too, so that runoff does not carry germs toward the herd.
- Use a solid fence or a wide gap, so the animals cannot touch noses. The Wisconsin guide adds that quarantined cattle should not even share the same airspace as the herd, to stop respiratory disease from spreading.
- Give it shade, a clean trough and feed, as you would any animal. A stressed animal in a bare, hot pen recovers slowly.
- Do not share feeders or troughs with the herd, which the Wisconsin guide also stresses. Keep the buckets, shovel, rope and halter for the quarantine pen separate. If you must share them, do the quarantine pen last and wash them after.
- Do not let manure from the quarantine pen wash toward your pasture or your cut-and-carry plot. Compost it separately, or ask the MAO about handling.

## Care for the new arrival

First, let it rest. Give clean water, and then feed in small amounts for the first day or two. Do not push it to eat.

Check it every day. The Wisconsin guide suggests taking the animal's temperature daily or at least every other day during quarantine, and calling the vet if it has a fever. Ask your vet to show you how, and what a normal reading is. If you do not have a thermometer, at least watch for these signs:

- Not eating, or eating much less than expected.
- Cough, nasal discharge or heavy breathing.
- Loose, watery or bloody manure.
- Lameness, swollen joints or sores around the mouth and feet.
- Lumps or scabs on the skin, and many ticks.
- Standing apart from others with its head down.

DA Caraga lists fever, blisters on the tongue and body, drooling and refusing feed as signs of foot and mouth disease. If you see blisters in the mouth or on the feet with drooling or limping, do not move any animal. Call your municipal or provincial veterinarian or BAI right away. Do not wait out the quarantine period before reporting.

## Talk to your vet

Quarantine is the best time to ask your veterinarian about vaccination and deworming. The DA Caraga guide says to make sure animals are properly vaccinated before they join the others, and the Wisconsin guide also suggests vaccinating during quarantine as your vet directs. A fecal exam can show which parasites the animal carries, so you treat only what is there. Do not give medicine by yourself and never guess a dose. Follow your vet's instructions and the product label, including any withdrawal period before slaughter.

Wisconsin's guide also suggests testing new animals for certain diseases during quarantine, but which diseases matter depends on the country. Ask your vet which tests make sense for the Philippines and your area.

## Dealing with ticks

New animals often bring ticks. A joint research project of BAI and the Queensland Department of Primary Industries, reported by Philstar in 2005, found that tick fever (babesiosis and anaplasmosis) occurs throughout the country. Local cattle have some natural resistance, but imported breeding stock is more at risk. Check the ears, neck, tail base and between the legs. Ask your vet what to use. Do not release the animal into the herd while it is carrying ticks.

## Visitors and vehicles

Quarantine is only one part of keeping disease out. Other ways in include:

- Trucks and vehicles that have carried other cattle. Ask for the vehicle to be washed before it loads your animals.
- Visitors, traders and neighbors who walk into your kural. Ask them to stay at the gate, or to wash and wear clean boots. Many diseases ride on boots and tires.
- Borrowed equipment, such as bulls, ropes or tools.
- Animals returning from a show, a paiwi arrangement or a market.

Treat returning animals as new ones. Put them in quarantine too.

## What to do at the end of the period

If the animal has stayed healthy for the full period, it can join the herd. Introduce it gradually, because cattle fight to establish their order. Do it in a large space with plenty of feed and water, and watch for the first few hours.

If it gets sick during quarantine, ask your vet what to do next and whether to start the count again once it recovers. Keep records of the date, the source and anything you saw. Those notes help if the animal turns out to be a problem.

## Summary

- Keep every newly bought animal in a separate pen before it joins the herd.
- DA Cordillera advises 28 days, and sources abroad say 30 days or longer. Ask your municipal veterinarian for the right period locally.
- Put the pen away from the herd, with no nose-to-nose contact, and use separate tools.
- Watch the animal every day for poor appetite, cough, loose manure, lameness and ticks.
- Report mouth or foot blisters at once, and give no medicine without a vet.
