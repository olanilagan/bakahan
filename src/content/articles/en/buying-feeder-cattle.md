The animal you buy decides most of your profit before you feed it a single bundle of grass. A feeder that arrives sick, too old or overpriced eats your margin from the first week. This guide covers what to check when you stand in front of the animal and the seller is waiting for your answer.

## Choose the season

The cattle fattening guides from DA Region 3 and DA Caraga both say feeder stock is usually cheaper in the dry season, January to April, when roads are easier and animals are easier to bring to market. Prices differ by province and by year, so treat that as a pattern to check, not a rule. Ask around your own trading post for a few weeks before you buy, so you know what a fair price per kilo looks like.

Buying in the dry season has a catch. Your own forage may be thin at the same time. Plan where the feed will come from before you bring the animal home.

## Decide what you are buying

Before you go to the trading post, write down three things: the weight you want, the breed or type, and the most you will pay. Without these, the seller sets the terms.

- Weight. The same DA guides describe backyard fattening as 120 to 180 days, with the animal reaching about 275 to 325 kg around six months after you buy it. Work backward from the weight you plan to sell at.
- Age. According to the DA guides, cattle of two to three years use feed more efficiently. Younger animals are cheaper because they are lighter, but they need a longer feeding period and better feed. Animals four years and older need less time in the kural. DA Caraga adds that younger cattle make sense when nutritious feed is plentiful, and older ones when grass and feed are limited.
- Sex. The DA guides prefer steers (castrated males) over heifers, because they are easier to handle and gain weight faster.
- Breed. The DA guides recommend tropical breeds such as Brahman, Ongole or Nellore and Indu-Brazil, because they adapt to our climate and feeds better than temperate breeds. They describe Brahman as resistant to disease and heat. "Batangas cattle" is not a separate breed. It is a name for cattle fattened in Batangas, many of them brought in from Mindoro, Masbate and other provinces.

The DA guides also suggest buying from a trusted breeder farm when you can.

## Check the animal itself

Look at the animal before you talk price, and look at it standing and walking. The DA guides describe a healthy animal as alert and active, with bright eyes and smooth skin and hair, and they say to avoid animals with rough or wrinkled skin, and any that are blind or lame. Walk around it slowly.

- Eyes. Clear and bright. Avoid animals with cloudy eyes, heavy discharge or signs of blindness.
- Coat and skin. Smooth and clean. Wrinkled, dull skin is a warning sign. Part the hair and look for ticks, especially around the ears, the neck and between the legs.
- Breathing and nose. Quiet breathing, a moist nose and no cough. Heavy breathing or runny discharge needs a second look.
- Legs and hooves. It should walk evenly, without limping. Check the mouth and hooves for sores, blisters or drooling. DA Caraga lists fever, blisters on the tongue and body, drooling and refusing feed as signs of foot and mouth disease. If you see these, walk away and report it to your municipal veterinarian.
- Manure. Loose, watery manure can be a sign of worms or illness.
- Behavior. An animal that stands apart from the group, with its head down, is usually unwell.

Be careful with a very fat animal and with a very thin one. A thin animal is not always a bargain, because it may be thin from sickness and not from short feed.

## Weigh it or estimate honestly

Cattle in places like Batangas are commonly priced per kilo of liveweight, as a UPLB study of the Batangas trade shows, so the scale decides how much you pay. If the trading post has a scale, use it and watch the reading. If not, a weighing tape is better than a guess. A seller who refuses any weighing and wants a flat price per head is asking you to trust his eye, so use your own.

Do the math before you agree. Multiply the weight by the price per kilo, then compare it with what you expect to get when you sell. Remember to include transport, feeds, vitamins, the vet and the days of labor, not only the purchase price.

## Ask for the papers

Under the Anti-Cattle Rustling Law of 1974 (PD 533), large cattle are registered with the city or municipal treasurer where they are raised, and the owner gets a certificate of ownership. Ask the seller for it. The same law requires a permit, the certificate of ownership and a veterinary health certificate before cattle are shipped from one province to another. How this is done today, and which offices issue what, differs by province and municipality, so ask your Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) or the municipal veterinarian what applies on your route. An animal sold with no papers and no known origin deserves questions.

A trader, sometimes called a *viajero*, may offer to arrange the papers for a fee. Ask what exactly you are paying for and get the receipt.

## Pay and move carefully

Agree on the price per kilo and the weighing before cash changes hands. Pay on the spot for the weight that was read, and write it down.

On the trip home, do not overload the vehicle. The DA guides warn that overloading causes lameness and bruising, and that rough handling causes stress and weight loss. They also suggest travelling at night. When you arrive, let the animal rest in a shaded kural with clean water and good grass, and do not push heavy feeding in the first days. A new animal is stressed, and stress lowers its resistance. The DA guides say not to mix new arrivals with your old animals, so keep it apart for a few weeks, because a sick feeder can infect the whole herd. Ask your vet which vaccines and deworming make sense for your area. Do not give any medicine yourself without that advice.

## Common mistakes

- Buying only because the price looks low.
- Skipping the scale and trusting the seller's guess.
- Buying a thin, sickly animal hoping feeds will fix it.
- Mixing new animals with the herd on the first day.
- Having no plan for feed, so the animal loses weight in the first month.

## Summary

- Buy in the dry season if you can, but plan your feed before the animal arrives.
- Decide the weight, the breed and your top price before you go to the trading post.
- A healthy feeder is alert, with clear eyes, a smooth coat and an even walk.
- Weigh the animal, compute the total cost and agree on the price per kilo in advance.
- Ask the MAO about permits and health papers, and keep the animal apart from your herd at first.
