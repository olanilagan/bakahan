The animal you buy decides most of your profit before you feed it a single bundle of grass. A feeder that arrives sick, too old or overpriced eats your margin from the first week. This guide covers what to check when you stand in front of the animal and the seller is waiting for your answer.

## Choose the season

According to a DA-linked guide, feeder stock usually comes cheaper in the dry season, roughly January to April, and country roads are easier to travel then. Prices differ by province and by year, so treat that as a pattern to check, not a rule. Ask around your own trading post for a few weeks before you buy, so you know what a fair price per kilo looks like.

Buying in the dry season has a catch. Your own forage may be thin at the same time. Plan where the feed will come from before you bring the animal home.

## Decide what you are buying

Before you go to the trading post, write down three things: the weight you want, the breed or type, and the most you will pay. Without these, the seller sets the terms.

- Weight. Many smallholders buy lighter animals and fatten them for several months. DA guidance cited in the Edge Davao report puts typical fattening at about 120 to 180 days, with animals ready at roughly 275 to 325 kg. Work backward from the weight you plan to sell at.
- Age. The DA guides say older animals, about 4 years and above, need less time in the kural. But if feed is plentiful, younger cattle are generally more economical to fatten. If your feed is limited, an older animal may suit you better.
- Breed. Tropical types such as Brahman and its crosses, and native or Batangas cattle, are recommended in DA material because they handle heat and tick-borne disease better. Imported temperate breeds usually struggle in lowland heat.

## Check the animal itself

Look at the animal before you talk price, and look at it standing and walking. A DA technoguide describes a healthy animal as alert and active, with clear eyes and a smooth, shiny coat. Walk around it slowly.

- Eyes. Clear and bright. Avoid animals with cloudy eyes, heavy discharge or signs of blindness.
- Coat and skin. Smooth and clean. Wrinkled, dull skin is a warning sign. Part the hair and look for ticks, especially around the ears, the neck and between the legs.
- Breathing and nose. Quiet breathing, a moist nose and no cough. Heavy breathing or runny discharge needs a second look.
- Legs and hooves. It should walk evenly, without limping. Check the mouth and hooves for sores, blisters or drooling. Blisters with drooling and limping are a reason to walk away and report it to your veterinarian.
- Manure. Loose, watery manure means worms or illness.
- Behavior. An animal that stands apart from the group, with its head down, is usually unwell.

Be careful with a very fat animal and with a very thin one. A thin animal is not always a bargain, because it may be thin from sickness and not from short feed.

## Weigh it or estimate honestly

Most sales are by liveweight at so many pesos per kilo, so the scale decides how much you pay. If the trading post has a scale, use it and watch the reading. If not, a weighing tape is better than a guess. A seller who refuses any weighing and wants a flat price per head is asking you to trust his eye, so use your own.

Do the math before you agree. Multiply the weight by the price per kilo, then compare it with what you expect to get when you sell. Remember to include transport, feeds, vitamins, the vet and the days of labor, not only the purchase price.

## Ask for the papers

Moving cattle usually needs papers, and the rules differ by province and by municipality. Ask the seller for proof of ownership and ask your Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) or the municipal veterinarian what permits and health certificates apply on your route. This matters more if the animal crosses provincial lines. Large cattle should also be registered under the anti-cattle rustling law, so an animal sold with no papers and no known origin deserves questions.

A trader, sometimes called a *viajero*, may offer to arrange the papers for a fee. Ask what exactly you are paying for and get the receipt.

## Pay and move carefully

Agree on the price per kilo and the weighing before cash changes hands. Pay on the spot for the weight that was read, and write it down.

On the trip home, do not overload the vehicle and avoid the midday sun. Give water when you arrive, then let the animal rest in a shaded kural before you feed it heavily. A new animal is stressed, and stress lowers its resistance. Keep it apart from your other cattle for a few weeks, because a sick feeder can infect the whole herd. Ask your vet which vaccines and deworming make sense for your area. Do not give any medicine yourself without that advice.

## Common mistakes

- Buying only because the price looks low.
- Skipping the scale and trusting the seller's guess.
- Buying a thin, sickly animal hoping feeds will fix it.
- Mixing new animals with the herd on the first day.
- Having no plan for feed, so the animal loses weight in the first month.

## Summary

- Buy in the dry season if you can, but plan your feed before the animal arrives.
- Decide the weight, the breed and your top price before you go to the trading post.
- A healthy feeder is alert, with clear eyes, a smooth coat and an even walk.
- Weigh the animal, compute the total cost and agree on the price per kilo in advance.
- Ask the MAO about permits and health papers, and keep the animal apart from your herd at first.
