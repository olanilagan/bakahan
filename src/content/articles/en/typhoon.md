Every year, typhoons and floods kill cattle that could have been saved. Many are lost because they were left tied in a low field, stuck in a flooded kural, or hit by a falling roof. A cattle farm that prepares before the rainy season, and acts early when a storm comes, usually loses far less.

## Reading PAGASA warnings

PAGASA raises Tropical Cyclone Wind Signals from No. 1 to No. 5 for the areas a storm will affect. Each signal comes with a lead time, which is roughly how long you have before the winds arrive.

| Signal | Wind speed | Lead time |
|---|---|---|
| No. 1 | 39 to 61 km/h | 36 hours |
| No. 2 | 62 to 88 km/h | 24 hours |
| No. 3 | 89 to 117 km/h | 18 hours |
| No. 4 | 118 to 184 km/h | 12 hours |
| No. 5 | 185 km/h or more | 12 hours |

According to PAGASA, light structures start getting damaged at Signal No. 1 and roofs of ordinary houses at No. 2. Many kural and feed sheds are light structures. So don't wait for No. 3 to act. Signal No. 1 is your cue to start moving animals and securing the farm.

Floods often come from rain, not wind, and can hit even without a high signal. PAGASA also issues rainfall warnings by color. Yellow means be alert for flooding in low-lying areas, orange means get ready, and red means act now. If your pasture or kural sits near a river or in a low area, an orange warning is reason enough to move the cattle.

Follow PAGASA's official page or the local radio, and listen to your barangay and MDRRMO.

## Before the rainy season

Do these early, while the weather is still good:

- Know where your high ground is. Pick a safe, elevated spot where the herd can stay for a few days, and the route to get there. If you don't have one, arrange with a neighbour or the barangay ahead of time.
- Check the kural roof and posts. Tie down or bolt loose GI sheets, and cut back branches that could fall on the shed.
- Clean the drainage canals around the kural and feed storage.
- Make sure every animal can be identified, through ear tags, brands or at least clear photos. After a flood, cattle get scattered and mixed with other herds. Records also help when you report losses or claim insurance.
- Keep the phone numbers of your MAO, your vet and the barangay where you can find them.

## When a storm is coming

The BAI advisory issued before Tropical Storm Uwan in 2025 gave simple steps for animal owners: move animals to safe, elevated areas away from flood zones, prepare an evacuation kit (feed, water, medicines, identification), don't leave animals tethered or exposed, and handle them humanely while moving them.

In practice, for a cattle raiser that means:

- Move the cattle to high ground while there is still daylight and the roads are passable. Moving scared cattle in wind and rain at night is dangerous for them and for you.
- Bring stored feed with them, or move it to a dry, raised spot. Dry feed like dayami (rice straw), hay and bagged concentrates keeps longer than fresh-cut grass.
- Fill drums or containers with clean water. Flood water and wells nearby can get contaminated.
- Keep vaccination records, papers and medicines in a waterproof bag.

## During the storm

The most common mistake is tying cattle by a short rope in an open field or inside a low kural. If the water rises, a tied animal cannot lift its head and drowns. In strong wind, it can get tangled and strangled. The BAI advisory says plainly to avoid leaving animals tethered.

If your cattle are on safe high ground, it is better to leave them loose in a fenced area, or tied with enough slack to move, than tied short. A sturdy windbreak like a line of trees or a solid wall helps. A weak shed whose roof could fly off can be more dangerous than open ground.

Don't go out to check animals while the storm is strong. Wait until the winds drop.

## After the storm

Once it is safe:

- Count your animals and check each one for wounds, limping, swelling or breathing trouble. Small wounds can get infected quickly in wet, dirty conditions. Call your vet for anything serious.
- Give clean water. Don't let cattle drink stagnant flood water if you can avoid it.
- Throw out feed that got soaked or shows mold. Wet concentrates and hay spoil fast, and moldy feed can make cattle sick.
- Check fences, since loose cattle wander onto roads and into neighbours' farms after storms.
- Bury or properly dispose of dead animals. Do not sell or eat the meat of animals that died. Ask your MAO or vet how to dispose of carcasses in your area.

## Watch for disease after floods

Hemorrhagic septicemia (HS) is a serious bacterial disease of cattle and carabao. The DA has listed it among the diseases of concern during the rainy season, and WOAH notes that outbreaks in Asia are linked to monsoon weather with high humidity and heat. WOAH describes it as acute and highly fatal, so if cattle suddenly fall sick or die after a flood, call your vet or MAO at once. HS is a notifiable disease, and early reporting protects your neighbours' herds too.

Ask your vet about vaccinating against HS before the rainy season, and follow their advice on timing. Our guide on keeping cattle healthy has more on HS and other common diseases.

## Reporting losses and getting help

Report dead, missing or injured animals to your MAO as soon as you can. The DA's assistance after a disaster usually goes through the agriculture offices, so being on their list matters. Make sure you're registered in the RSBSA, the DA's farmer registry, and ask the MAO what is available.

After Uwan in 2025, for example, the DA announced feeds and supplements for livestock and poultry, a SURE loan of up to ₱25,000 through the Agricultural Credit Policy Council, and indemnity through the Philippine Crop Insurance Corporation (PCIC) for insured farmers. The terms change from one disaster to the next, so check with your MAO.

PCIC also offers livestock insurance for cattle. Its guidelines list typhoon and flood among the perils that can be covered. Premiums, the amount insured and eligibility change over time, so ask your MAO or the nearest PCIC office before the rainy season starts.

## Summary

- Act at Signal No. 1 or an orange rainfall warning. Don't wait for the wind.
- Pick your high ground and the route to it before the rainy season.
- Never leave cattle tied short where water can rise or wind can tangle them.
- After the storm, check for wounds, give clean water and throw out spoiled feed.
- Call the vet fast if cattle suddenly fall sick after a flood, and ask about HS vaccination before the rains.
- Report losses to your MAO, stay registered in the RSBSA, and look into PCIC insurance.
