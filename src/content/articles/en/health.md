A sick animal costs you twice: once for the medicine, and again for the weight it doesn't gain. In fattening, the profit is often only a few kilos per head, so keeping your cattle healthy is the main job, not an extra one. This guide covers how to spot trouble early, the problems that hit Philippine cattle most, and the daily habits that keep disease out of your bakahan.

## Checking your animals every day

Your best tool is your own eyes, every morning and every afternoon. Learn how each animal normally acts. Then you will notice right away when something changes.

### Signs of a healthy animal

Department of Agriculture guidance on choosing feeder cattle describes a healthy animal as:

- alert and active, aware of what is going on around it
- bright, clear eyes
- a smooth, shiny coat
- a moist muzzle (the nose area)
- walking normally, with no limp

On top of that, a healthy animal eats and chews its cud, drinks normally, and has normal manure for what it's being fed.

### Warning signs

Look closer, and think about calling a vet, if an animal:

- stays away from the group, head down, dull or weak
- stops eating or stops chewing its cud
- has a rough, dull coat
- limps or doesn't want to move
- drools a lot, or has sores or blisters on the mouth, nose, tongue or feet
- has a swollen belly, especially on the **left** side
- has diarrhea, dark urine, fast or hard breathing, or a fever
- keeps losing weight even though you are feeding it

## Common health problems in Philippine cattle

### Hemorrhagic septicemia (HS)

UPLB researchers call hemorrhagic septicemia the principal killer disease of ruminants in the Philippines, especially cattle and carabao. A bacterium causes it (*Pasteurella multocida*), and the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) describes it as acute and highly fatal. In Asia, outbreaks mostly come in hot, humid, monsoon-type weather, so watch your animals more closely during the rainy season.

HS can kill fast, so vaccination is your protection. DA guidance says feeder animals should be vaccinated before they go into the feedlot. Ask your vet or your city or municipal agriculture office about HS vaccination in your area.

### Worms and liver fluke

Worms in the stomach and gut, and liver fluke (fasciolosis), eat into your profit without much warning. The animal eats but doesn't gain well.

- A Visayas State University study of slaughtered animals in Ormoc City found liver fluke in about 18% of cattle and 59% of carabaos.
- The Philippine Carabao Center says fasciolosis is one of the leading causes of the decline of carabaos and other ruminants.

Liver fluke is linked with wet, swampy places, so it matters where your animals graze and drink. Regular deworming is the usual answer, but the right product and timing depend on your area. A vet can run a fecal (manure) exam to find out which parasites are really there before you treat.

### Ticks and tick fever

Ticks are more than a nuisance. Research by the Bureau of Animal Industry with Australian partners found that babesiosis and anaplasmosis, together called tick fever, occur all over the Philippines. Local cattle tend to resist it better. But the researchers warned that improved or imported breeds can be more susceptible, and in those animals tick fever can cause big losses and deaths.

Check your animals for ticks regularly, especially around the ears, neck, belly and tail. Ask your vet which tick control suits your animals.

### Bloat

Bloat is gas trapped in the rumen (the big first stomach). Watch for:

- a swollen upper left side of the belly, which is the first and most important sign
- kicking at the belly, lying down and getting up again and again
- grunting, stretching the neck, hard breathing

Bloat can kill within hours, so **call a vet right away**. To prevent it, change rations slowly. University of Nebraska guidance says to move cattle from forage-based to grain-based rations gradually, over at least three weeks.

### Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD)

The Philippines has kept its WOAH-recognized status as FMD-free without vaccination since May 2015. That status protects our herds and our trade, so it is worth guarding.

According to WOAH, FMD causes fever, blisters on the tongue, lips, nose, between the toes and on the teats, heavy drooling, and painful sores that make animals lame. It spreads through the air, on contaminated vehicles, clothes, footwear and equipment, and through contaminated meat or animal products fed to animals.

If you ever see **blisters in the mouth or on the feet together with drooling and limping, do not move any animals**. Report it right away to your municipal or provincial veterinarian or to the Bureau of Animal Industry (BAI).

## Quarantine for new animals

Most diseases walk into a farm on four legs. Before you add new animals:

- Buy from sources you trust, and check the animals against the healthy signs above.
- Keep new arrivals apart from your herd, in their own pen with their own feed and water, for as long as your vet recommends.
- Watch them closely every day while they are in quarantine.
- Vaccinate and deworm them as your vet advises before they join the herd.
- Start them on roughage with only a little concentrate. DA guidance says the first weeks in the feedlot are critical.

### Papers for moving animals

Moving cattle from one place to another needs papers, usually a Veterinary Health Certificate and a shipping permit. BAI's National Veterinary Quarantine Services Division processes local shipping permits for live animals online, and permits are checked at BAI quarantine checkpoints. These rules exist because uncontrolled movement of animals is one of the fastest ways diseases like FMD spread. Ask your provincial or municipal veterinarian what you need before you buy or sell.

## Biosecurity on the farm

Biosecurity just means keeping disease out, and stopping it from spreading if it gets in.

- Limit visitors. Fewer people and vehicles near your kural (pen) means fewer chances to bring in germs.
- Have a footbath or a place to wash boots at the entrance, and clean your tools.
- Keep vehicles away from the animals where you can. A truck that visits other farms can carry disease.
- Keep pens, feed troughs and water troughs clean.
- **Never feed your animals leftover meat or food scraps that contain meat.**
- Separate sick animals from healthy ones right away.
- Write down every vaccination, deworming, treatment and death.

## Vaccination and deworming schedules

We don't give a fixed schedule or drug doses here, on purpose. The right program depends on your location, the season, the animals' history and which diseases are around. Ask:

- your private veterinarian
- your city or municipal agriculture office or municipal veterinarian (LGU)
- your provincial veterinarian or the BAI

Always follow the label and your vet's instructions on dose and on the **withdrawal period** (how long to wait after treatment before you can sell the animal for slaughter).

## When to call a veterinarian

Call right away if you see:

- sudden death of any animal
- blisters in the mouth or on the feet, with drooling or limping
- a swollen left belly (possible bloat)
- high fever, hard breathing, or an animal that collapses
- several animals getting sick at the same time
- an animal that hasn't eaten for more than a day

If you're not sure, call. A vet visit costs far less than losing an animal.

## Summary

- Check your animals morning and afternoon. A healthy one is alert, bright-eyed, smooth-coated and has a moist muzzle.
- HS, worms, liver fluke, ticks and bloat cause most losses, and preventing them costs less than treating them.
- The Philippines is FMD-free without vaccination. Follow the rules on moving animals and report blisters with drooling and limping right away.
- Quarantine new animals, limit visitors, clean boots and tools, and separate the sick.
- Get vaccination and deworming schedules from your vet, LGU or BAI. Never guess doses.
