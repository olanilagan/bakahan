Every head of cattle in your *kural* leaves a pile of dung behind every day. Left alone, it draws flies and the neighbours start to complain. Handled well, it feeds your Napier plot and cuts what you spend on fertilizer. Some raisers even sell it.

## How much manure to expect

Cattle produce a lot. NDSU Extension in the United States estimates that a beef cow of about 450 kilos (1,000 pounds) kept in confinement produces about 42 kilos (92 pounds) of manure a day, and a finishing calf of about 340 kilos (750 pounds) about 17 kilos (37 pounds). Those figures come from American feedlot data and likely include urine, so treat them only as a rough guide. Our native and crossbred cattle are usually lighter, and animals that graze part of the day leave much of it in the field.

Even so, a few head in a *kural* will fill a wheelbarrow or more each day. Plan where it goes before you buy the animals.

## Collecting and storing it

The DA Region III guide on backyard fattening reminds raisers to clean out manure regularly, because dirty pens can lower an animal's weight gain and appetite. A concrete floor with a slight slope makes this much easier.

- Scrape or shovel out the pen at least once a day.
- Pile the manure in one spot, away from the pen, the house and any well or creek.
- Keep the pile covered. The ATI composting guide uses laminated plastic sacks to keep rainwater out. Rain washes nutrients away and turns the pile into a smelly puddle.
- Mix in dry material like *dayami* (rice straw), dried leaves or sawdust if the pile is too wet.

## Simple heap composting

Composting turns fresh manure into a dark, crumbly fertilizer that is easier to handle and smells much less. The ATI and Philippine Coconut Authority guide on bio-organic fertilizer gives a method most farms can copy.

1. Pick a shaded, well-drained spot near water. The guide says 4 tons of farm waste fits in an area about 4 by 6 meters.
2. Mix plant material (rice straw, coir dust, bagasse, mudpress or weeds) with manure. The guide allows ratios of 1:1, 2:1 or 1:2 of plant material to manure. Its example uses chicken and pig manure, but cattle manure works the same way.
3. Build it in layers. Water each layer until a squeezed handful is wet but doesn't drip.
4. Sprinkle a compost activator such as *Trichoderma* on each layer if you have it. Ask your MAO or ATI where to get one locally.
5. Cover the heap and leave it for 4 to 7 days, then turn and mix it. Turn it again every week.
6. Harvest after three to four weeks. Ready compost is dark brown to black with no bad smell, and the heap has cooled down.

The guide says an activator speeds things up from about three months to just three to four weeks. Without one, the same heap still works, just slower.

## Vermicomposting with African nightcrawlers

Vermicomposting uses earthworms to break down manure. In the Philippines the usual worm is the African nightcrawler (*Eudrilus eugeniae*). Research on it started at Central Luzon State University in the 1970s, according to a 2008 BioCycle article by R.D. Guerrero III.

That article describes the basic method:

- Mix about 25% shredded rice straw with 75% rotted manure.
- Let the mix decompose for 2 to 3 weeks under cover before adding worms. Fresh manure heats up and can kill them.
- Stock about 1 kilo of worms per square meter of bed, with the bed about 30 cm deep.
- Keep beds shaded and moist, and protect them from predators.
- Harvest after about 4 to 6 weeks, then air-dry the vermicast in the shade.

A UPLB study also used cattle manure mixed with plant residues as worm feed. You get vermicast for your plots and extra worms, which you can use to start more beds.

## Using compost on your forage

Spread compost or vermicast on your Napier and other forage plots before planting and after cutting. It improves the soil and holds water better in *tag-init*.

Composted manure releases its nitrogen slowly. NDSU Extension says fresh solid manure makes about half its nitrogen available in the first season, while composted manure makes only about 20% available. So compost builds the soil over time, but you may still need some commercial fertilizer for fast growth. The ATI guide suggests starting with bio-organic fertilizer combined with half the usual rate of inorganic fertilizer. Ask your MAO for advice on your soil.

## Selling organic fertilizer

Selling sacks of dried manure or compost to nearby vegetable growers and plant sellers is an easy start. Ask around to see what people in your area pay.

If you want to sell bagged organic fertilizer as a real product, you'll deal with the Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority (FPA). The ATI guide notes that to be registered with the FPA as pure organic fertilizer, a product must have 5 to 7% total NPK, with at least 1.5% nitrogen and at least 10% carbon. Contact the FPA regional office about registration and licensing before you sell under a brand name.

## Small biogas digesters

A biogas digester is a sealed tank or bag where manure breaks down without air and gives off gas you can cook with. The leftover slurry can go on your crops.

The BAI developed a low-cost tubular polyethylene digester for backyard raisers. A 1997 paper by Moog and others describes its use mainly with pigs. Users interviewed for the paper saved about ₱160 a month on cooking fuel at the time, and farmers were encouraged to put the slurry on rice and vegetables. Cattle manure can be used too, but the paper's numbers are for pigs and are now old.

There is also a Philippine standard for farm biogas plants, PNS/PAES 413:2001. Before building one, ask your MAO, the BAI or a DOST office for current designs and costs. Biogas burns, so keep flames and cigarettes away from the digester and pipes.

## Being a good neighbour

Smell and flies from a cattle pen are an easy way to fall out with the people next door.

- Clean pens daily to keep flies down.
- Keep piles covered and away from the property line.
- Never let manure water drain into a well, creek, irrigation canal or someone else's lot.
- Spread manure away from water sources, and not just before heavy rain.

Rules on setbacks and waste differ by town. Check with your MAO or the municipal environment office before you expand.

## Summary

- Cattle make a lot of manure. Plan where it goes before you buy animals.
- Clean pens daily and keep the manure pile covered and away from water.
- Heap composting with plant material is ready in about a month with an activator, or about three months without.
- African nightcrawlers turn pre-composted manure into vermicast in about 4 to 6 weeks.
- Compost builds your forage soil slowly. You may still need some commercial fertilizer.
- Ask the FPA before selling branded fertilizer, and the MAO or BAI before building a biogas digester.
