A cow-calf herd earns from calves, so everything depends on getting your cows pregnant on time and keeping them that way. You don't need a big farm for this. Mostly you need to watch your cows closely and write things down.

This guide covers the basics for a small herd. It applies just as well if you keep cows under *paiwi* (where you raise someone else's cattle and share the calves). For choosing breeds, see our breeds guide. For anything involving medicines or a sick animal, talk to a licensed veterinarian.

## When a heifer is ready

Breed a heifer (a young female that hasn't calved yet) by size and condition, not age alone. Bred too young and small, she can be stunted and have a hard calving.

- The DA's cattle breeding guidelines (Administrative Order No. 14, 1994) say heifers are bred at not less than 20 months of age.
- Extension services abroad use a weight target: about 65% of her expected mature body weight at first breeding, according to the Alabama Cooperative Extension System.
- The same source notes that Brahman and Brahman-type cattle usually reach puberty later than British breeds. Many of our cattle carry Brahman blood, so don't be surprised if your heifers take longer.

So if your mature cows weigh around 350 kilos, aim for roughly 230 kilos before a heifer's first breeding. Ask your MAO livestock technician if you're unsure.

## Reading the signs of heat

A cow can only get pregnant when she is in heat (estrus). Miss it and you wait another cycle. These are approximate figures. The averages for the heat cycle and heat length come from the BAI and ATI leaflet on artificial insemination:

| What | Average | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Heat cycle (one heat to the next) | about 21 days | 18 to 24 days |
| How long heat lasts | about 18 hours | 10 to 24 hours |
| Pregnancy length | about 283 days | 273 to 290 days |

The cycle range and pregnancy length come from the Alabama extension guide, which also lists heat lasting anywhere from 6 to 30 hours. It adds that Brahman-type cattle often carry a little longer.

Signs to watch for, from the BAI leaflet:

- She stands still when another animal mounts her. This "standing heat" is the surest sign.
- A clear mucus discharge from the vulva.
- The vulva is swollen and reddish.

The Alabama guide adds restlessness and mounting other cattle. Watch for at least 30 minutes, twice a day. Early morning and late afternoon are the best times. If your cattle are tethered along the road during the day, check them when you move them and again at the *kural* in the evening.

## Bull or artificial insemination

With natural mating, you either keep a bull or borrow one from a neighbour. It's simple, and the bull finds the cows in heat for you. But a bull has to be fed, can be dangerous, and passes his traits to every calf, good or bad. A borrowed bull can also bring reproductive diseases from other herds.

AI means a trained technician places semen from a selected bull into the cow. The BAI leaflet lists these advantages:

- access to semen from high-quality bulls (Brahman, Red Brahman, Simbrah, Wagyu and others are offered)
- service even when no good bull is nearby
- less spread of reproductive diseases
- no cost or risk of keeping your own bull

AI only works if you catch the heat. The leaflet's rule of thumb: a cow seen in heat in the morning is inseminated that afternoon, and one seen in heat in the afternoon is inseminated the next morning. Call the technician as soon as you notice the signs. The leaflet also asks for a breeding chute or rack where the cow can be held safely.

## Where to get AI service

AI for cattle is run under the Unified National Artificial Insemination Program (UNAIP), with BAI's National Artificial Breeding Center in Malaybalay, Bukidnon. The BAI and ATI leaflet tells farmers to go to:

- your City or Municipal Agriculture Office
- the Provincial or City Veterinary or Agriculture Office
- the DA Regional Field Office
- the AI technician assigned to your area

Get the technician's name and mobile number before your cows come into heat, not after. Fees and availability differ by town, so ask your MAO.

## Checking if she's pregnant

Don't just wait nine months to find out. A veterinarian or trained technician can check pregnancy by feeling the uterus through the rectum (palpation) or by ultrasound. According to a 2023 UF/IFAS Extension guide, palpation can confirm pregnancy about 35 to 40 days after breeding, and ultrasound about 28 days after.

Knowing early lets you rebreed an empty cow on her next heat, or decide whether to keep her. Ask your municipal or provincial veterinarian about pregnancy checks. Leave this job to a trained person.

## Feeding the pregnant cow

A pregnant cow needs steady, good feeding, especially near calving and while nursing. Oregon State University Extension says cows should calve and go back into breeding in at least moderate body condition, and warns that over-fat cows also have breeding problems. Too thin and she may not come back into heat for a long time after calving.

In practice, give her good forage, clean water and minerals every day, and add better feed if she's losing condition during the dry months. Our feeds guide covers forage and supplements. Ask your vet before giving any vitamins or medicines during pregnancy.

## Aiming for one calf a year

The usual goal is one calf per cow per year. Oregon State Extension says a calving interval (the days between two calvings) of no more than 365 days is desirable. With a pregnancy of about 283 days, that leaves her roughly 80 days after calving to get pregnant again.

That's tight. Body condition at calving matters most, because a thin cow is slow to return to heat. If a cow keeps going well past a year between calves, talk to your vet first, then think about replacing her.

The DA's 1994 guidelines set a breeding season of April 16 to June 15 for government herds, so calves arrive from late December to February. You don't have to follow it, but breeding in one season makes heat watching and calving easier to manage.

## Keeping breeding records

The DA's breeding guidelines ask for complete breeding records: heats, AI services including the semen used, and calving dates. A notebook is enough. For each cow, write down:

- her name or ear tag number
- each date you saw her in heat
- each breeding date, and whether it was a bull or AI (with the bull or semen name)
- the pregnancy check result and date
- expected calving date (count about 283 days from breeding)
- actual calving date, the calf's sex and any problems

After a year or two, your records will show which cows are eating without giving you calves.

## Summary

- Breed heifers by size and condition. The DA says not less than 20 months, and a common target is about 65% of mature weight.
- Heat comes about every 21 days and usually lasts less than a day. Watch twice a day, mornings and late afternoons.
- AI gives you better bulls without keeping one. Get the AI technician's number from your MAO before you need it.
- Have a vet or trained technician confirm pregnancy about a month or more after breeding.
- Aim for one calf per cow per year, and keep cows in good condition so they rebreed on time.
- Write down every heat, breeding, pregnancy check and calving.
