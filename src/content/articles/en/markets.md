Raising a good animal is only half the job. The other half is selling it at a fair price, or buying the right animal without getting cheated. Here most cattle still change hands face to face, at livestock markets, through viajeros (travelling traders) and between neighbours, so this guide covers how that works and which papers you need.

## Padre Garcia, Batangas

Ask anyone in the cattle business where the trading happens and they will likely say Padre Garcia, Batangas. The town calls itself the "Cattle Trading Capital of the Philippines" because of its livestock auction market.

This is what the municipality itself says about the market:

- Trading started there in the 1950s, with traders coming from Pangasinan, Masbate, Marinduque and the Bicol region.
- The town estimates that about 300 traders do business there, from Marinduque, Mindoro, Romblon, Bicol, Pangasinan, CALABARZON and Metro Manila.
- The town's pages say the weekly auction draws visitors on Thursdays and Fridays, and is busiest on Friday mornings. Schedules can change, so confirm with the municipal office or someone who trades there before you make the trip.
- It is mostly cattle, but carabao, horses, pigs and goats are sold too.
- Despite the name "auction," the town says prices are agreed by negotiation between buyer and seller, not by open bidding.
- Because there are so many animals, the town says weighing each one isn't practical. Many locals estimate by eye ("mata-mata") to speed up the haggling.

## Other livestock markets (oksyon)

Padre Garcia is the most famous, but there are others. Many provinces and towns run a livestock oksyon, often on a fixed day each week.

The Bureau of Animal Industry (BAI) sets rules for accredited oksyon markets. Under BAI Administrative Order No. 16 (2004), an accredited market should have:

- a scale house with a working weighing scale
- pens, loading ramps and a fence
- a market manager, a livestock inspector or veterinarian, and a weigher
- regular inspection by BAI veterinary quarantine officers

The same order says the weigher should weigh each animal coming in and give the seller a weighing slip, and the market office prepares transfer certificates for cattle, carabao and horses.

Some markets are coming back. Batac City in Ilocos Norte reopened its traditional "Dayo" livestock auction at a new site in June 2026. To find the market nearest you, ask your municipal agriculturist or provincial veterinarian.

## Traders and viajeros

A big share of cattle is bought by traders, usually called viajeros. They travel between farms, towns and markets, then resell to other raisers, fatteners or butchers.

Viajeros are useful. They bring buyers to farms far from town and save you hauling costs. They earn on the difference between what they pay you and what they sell for. That's fair, as long as you know what your animal is worth first.

Under PD 533 (the Anti-Cattle Rustling Law), anyone in the business of buying and selling large cattle needs a permit. BAI also requires livestock handlers who move animals between provinces to be licensed. A real, regular trader should have no problem showing you their papers.

## Knowing a fair price

### Liveweight or mata-mata

There are two common ways to price cattle:

| Method | How it works | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liveweight (per kilo) | Animal is weighed on a scale; price = weight × agreed price per kilo | Clear, easy to check, fair to both sides | Scale must be accurate; animal should not be overfed or watered just before weighing |
| Mata-mata (by eye) | Buyer and seller agree on one price for the whole animal | Fast; common in busy markets | Experienced traders are usually much better at guessing than smallholders |

If you are new, sell by liveweight. When you sell by eye, whoever guesses better wins, and that is usually the experienced viajero.

### Getting a fair deal

- **Know the going rate.** The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) publishes regular reports on the farmgate price of cattle, in pesos per kilo liveweight. Use it as a rough guide, then ask other raisers and traders what animals are actually selling for in your area this month.
- **Weigh before you sell.** Use the scale at a market, a cooperative or a neighbour's place. Even if you end up selling by eye, knowing the weight helps you bargain.
- **No scale?** A weight tape (the kind you wrap around the chest, behind the front legs) gives a rough estimate. It's not as accurate as a scale but much better than guessing.
- **Don't sell in a panic.** Raisers who have to sell fast often get less. Plan when you will sell, if you can.

## Papers for selling and moving cattle

Cattle are valuable and easy to steal, so the law requires papers. The rules come from national law (PD 533), BAI orders and your local government, and the details can differ by province. These are the main documents.

### Certificate of ownership

Under PD 533, owners must register large cattle with the city or municipal treasurer where the animals are raised, **before the animal turns six months old**. The LGU then issues a certificate of ownership. "Large cattle" in the law covers cows, carabao, horses and similar animals.

### Certificate of transfer

When an animal is sold, the transfer is recorded and the new owner gets a certificate of transfer, usually through the municipal treasurer's office. Accredited oksyon markets also prepare transfer certificates. Fees are set locally, so ask at your town hall.

### Veterinary health certificate (VHC)

A VHC confirms the animal is healthy and where it came from. Under BAI rules, any licensed veterinarian can issue it, and you need it before you can get a shipping permit. PD 533 also requires a certification from the Provincial Veterinarian that the animals are disease-free before a shipment permit is issued.

### Shipping or transport permit

- **Within the same province:** PD 533 says you need a permit from the city or municipal treasurer of the place the animal comes from.
- **To another province:** You need a shipping permit. BAI rules say deputized veterinary quarantine officers issue these.
- **PNP clearance:** BAI rules also require a PNP clearance to ship or transport large animals like cattle and carabao.

Traders who move animals between provinces also need a livestock handler's license from BAI, and trucks or boats carrying livestock should be accredited by BAI.

**Under PD 533, failing to show the required papers when asked can be taken as evidence that the cattle were stolen.** Always travel with your documents. Requirements change, so before moving animals, check with your municipal treasurer, MAO (Municipal Agriculture Office) or provincial veterinary office.

## Avoiding cheats and stolen cattle

- Always ask for the certificate of ownership and check that the name matches the seller. If the seller says they are "selling for a relative," be extra careful.
- Check ear tags, brands or marks against the papers.
- Be wary of a price that is too good to be true, or a seller in a hurry.
- Do the transfer properly at the municipal treasurer's office or the market office. Keep the receipts and weighing slips.
- Buy from people you know, or at an accredited market where staff check papers.
- Look at the animal's health before you pay, and ask for the VHC. One sick animal brought onto your farm can infect the rest.
- Agree on everything before loading: price, how it will be weighed, who pays for hauling and permits, and when you get paid.

## Summary

- Padre Garcia, Batangas is the best-known cattle market in the country. The town says it is busiest on Thursdays and Fridays, but confirm the schedule before you go.
- Many deals are made mata-mata, by eye. If you're not experienced, insist on weighing.
- Check PSA farmgate prices and ask around locally so you know what your animal is worth.
- Register your cattle with the municipal treasurer before six months of age and keep the certificate of ownership safe.
- To move animals you generally need a VHC, a shipping or transport permit, and a PNP clearance for large animals. Rules differ by place, so ask your LGU and provincial vet.
- Never buy cattle without proper ownership papers. Under the law, missing papers can be taken as a sign of theft.
