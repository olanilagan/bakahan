Raising a good animal is only half the job. The other half is selling it at a fair price, or buying the right animal without getting burned. In the Philippines, most cattle still change hands face to face: at livestock auction markets, through traders, and between neighbors. This guide explains how it works, what papers you need, and how to protect yourself.

## Padre Garcia: the country's best-known cattle market

If you ask anyone in the cattle business where the trading happens, they will likely say **Padre Garcia, Batangas**. The town calls itself the "Cattle Trading Capital of the Philippines," and its livestock auction market is the reason.

What the municipality itself says about the market:

- **It is old.** Trading started in the 1950s, with traders coming from Pangasinan, Masbate, Marinduque and the Bicol region.
- **It draws traders from far away.** The town estimates about 300 traders do business there, from Marinduque, Mindoro, Romblon, Bicol, Pangasinan, CALABARZON and Metro Manila.
- **Thursday and Friday are the big days.** The town's pages say the weekly auction draws visitors on Thursdays and Fridays, and is busiest on Friday mornings. Schedules can change, so confirm with the municipal office or someone who trades there before you make the trip.
- **It is mostly cattle,** but carabao, horses, pigs and goats are sold too.
- **Prices are agreed, not bid.** Despite the name "auction," the town says deals are made by negotiation between buyer and seller rather than open bidding.
- **Most deals use "mata-mata."** Because of the volume, the town notes that weighing every animal is not practical, so many locals estimate by eye to speed up negotiation.

## Other livestock markets ("oksyon") around the country

Padre Garcia is the most famous, but it is not the only one. Many provinces and towns run a livestock **oksyon** market, often on a fixed weekly day.

The Bureau of Animal Industry (BAI) sets rules for accredited oksyon markets. Under BAI Administrative Order No. 16 (2004), an accredited market should have:

- a **scale house with a working weighing scale**
- pens, loading ramps and a fence
- a market manager, a livestock inspector or veterinarian, and a weigher
- regular inspection by BAI veterinary quarantine officers

The same order says the weigher should weigh each entering animal and give the seller a **weighing slip**, and the market office prepares **transfer certificates** for cattle, carabao and horses.

Some markets are being revived. Batac City in Ilocos Norte reopened its traditional "Dayo" livestock auction at a new site in June 2026. To find the nearest market to you, ask your municipal agriculturist or provincial veterinarian.

## Traders and viajeros

A big share of cattle is bought by **traders**, often called **viajeros** — buyers who travel between farms, towns and markets, then resell to other raisers, fatteners, or butchers.

Traders are useful: they bring buyers to remote farms and save you hauling costs. They earn on the difference between what they pay you and what they sell for. That's fair — just know what your animal is worth first.

Under PD 533 (the Anti-Cattle Rustling Law), anyone in the **business** of buying and selling large cattle needs a permit, and BAI also requires livestock handlers who move animals between provinces to be licensed. A legitimate regular trader should not mind showing you their papers.

## How to judge a fair price

### Liveweight vs. "mata-mata"

There are two common ways to price cattle:

| Method | How it works | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Liveweight (per kilo)** | Animal is weighed on a scale; price = weight × agreed price per kilo | Clear, easy to check, fair to both sides | Scale must be accurate; animal should not be overfed or watered just before weighing |
| **Mata-mata (by eye)** | Buyer and seller agree on one price for the whole animal | Fast; common in busy markets | Experienced traders are usually much better at guessing than smallholders |

If you are new, **liveweight is your friend**. When you sell by eye, the person who guesses better — usually the experienced viajero — wins.

### Tips for getting a fair deal

- **Know the going rate.** The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) publishes regular reports on the farmgate price of cattle, in pesos per kilo of liveweight. Use it as a rough guide, then ask other raisers and traders what animals are actually selling for in your area this month.
- **Weigh before you sell.** Use the scale at a market, a cooperative, or a neighbor's. Even if you sell by eye, knowing the weight helps you negotiate.
- **If you have no scale,** a weight tape (heart-girth tape) gives a rough estimate. It is not as accurate as a scale but much better than guessing.
- **Don't sell in a panic.** Raisers who must sell quickly often get lower prices. Plan your selling time when you can.

## Papers for selling and moving cattle

Cattle are valuable and easy to steal, so the law requires paperwork. Rules come from national law (PD 533), BAI orders, and your local government, and the details can vary by province. Here are the main documents:

### Certificate of ownership (registration)

Under PD 533, owners must **register large cattle with the city or municipal treasurer** where the animals are raised, **before the animal turns six months old**. The LGU then issues a **certificate of ownership**. "Large cattle" in the law includes cows, carabao, horses and similar animals.

### Certificate of transfer

When an animal is sold, the transfer is recorded and a **certificate of transfer** is issued to the new owner, usually through the municipal treasurer's office. Accredited oksyon markets also prepare transfer certificates. Fees are set locally — check with your town hall.

### Veterinary health certificate (VHC)

A VHC confirms the animal is healthy and where it came from. Under BAI rules, **any licensed veterinarian** may issue it, and it is needed before you can get a shipping permit. PD 533 also requires a certification from the Provincial Veterinarian that the animals are disease-free before a shipment permit is issued.

### Shipping/transport permit

- **Within the same province:** PD 533 says you need a permit from the city or municipal treasurer of the place of origin.
- **To another province:** You need a shipping permit. BAI rules say these are issued by deputized veterinary quarantine officers.
- **PNP clearance:** BAI rules also require a PNP clearance to ship or transport large animals like cattle and carabao.

Traders who move animals between provinces also need a **livestock handler's license** from BAI, and trucks or boats carrying livestock should be **accredited by BAI**.

**Important:** Under PD 533, failing to show the required papers when asked can be treated as evidence that the cattle were stolen. Always travel with your documents. Requirements change, so before moving animals, check with your municipal treasurer, municipal agriculture office, or provincial veterinary office.

## How to avoid being cheated or buying stolen cattle

- **Always ask for the certificate of ownership** and check that the name matches the seller. If the seller is "selling for a relative," be extra careful.
- **Check ear tags, brands or marks** against the papers.
- **Be wary of prices that are too good to be true** or sellers in a hurry.
- **Get the transfer done properly** at the municipal treasurer's office or the market office. Keep receipts and weighing slips.
- **Buy from people you know or at an accredited market,** where staff check papers.
- **Look at the animal's health** before paying. Ask for the VHC. A sick animal brought into your farm can infect your whole herd.
- **Agree on everything before loading:** price, weight method, who pays for hauling and permits, and when payment is made.

## Key takeaways

- Padre Garcia, Batangas is the best-known cattle market in the country; the town says trading is busiest on Thursdays and Fridays, but confirm the schedule before you go.
- Many deals are made "mata-mata," by eye. If you're not experienced, insist on weighing — liveweight protects you.
- Check PSA farmgate prices and ask around locally so you know what your animal is worth.
- Register your cattle with the municipal treasurer before six months of age and keep the certificate of ownership safe.
- To move animals, you generally need a VHC, a shipping or transport permit, and for large animals a PNP clearance. Rules differ by place, so ask your LGU and provincial vet.
- Never buy cattle without proper ownership papers — under the law, missing papers can be taken as a sign of theft.
