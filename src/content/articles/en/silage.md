In the rainy season the Napier grows faster than the cattle can eat it. By March or April the same field is dry and brown, and you are buying dayami or cutting grass along the road. Silage is how you keep the extra grass from the rainy months and feed it during tag-init.

## What silage is

Silage is chopped green forage packed tightly into a container with no air, so it ferments instead of rotting. The Philippine Carabao Center (PCC) calls the container a silo and the process ensiling. The souring keeps the feed for months. It does not make poor grass better, so start with good material.

PCC gives an example of how much you need. A 500-kg animal eats about 10% of its weight in fresh grass a day, or about 50 kg. If grass is short for six months (PCC uses January to June), that one animal needs roughly 9,000 kg of silage. Treat these as planning figures and adjust for your own animals and your own dry months.

## Crops you can use

PCC lists Napier, para grass, guinea grass, corn and sorghum as good silage crops, plus farm leftovers like corn stover and sugarcane tops. Legumes such as ipil-ipil, kakawate and rensonii can be added for protein.

Corn is the easiest. The ears carry plenty of sugar, which the silage bacteria need, and PCAARRD notes that corn silage holds its quality well in storage. Napier works too, but it is wetter and has less sugar, so it benefits more from wilting and from a little molasses.

## When to harvest

PCC gives these harvest times:

- Napier and other grasses: about 45 to 55 days from planting or from the last cut
- Corn: about 75 to 80 days, when the kernels are about two-thirds of the way along the milk line (PCAARRD)

Moisture matters. PCC aims for about 65 to 70% moisture in the material. If the grass is very wet, for example cut right after rain, spread it out and let it wilt for one to two days before chopping. Wet silage tends to rot and leak juice.

Cut a little above the ground so you don't pick up soil. Soil in silage brings in the wrong bacteria.

## Chopping

PCC recommends chopping to about 1 to 2 cm. Short pieces pack tighter and squeeze out more air. A forage chopper is fastest, but an itak works for small batches if you have helpers. Chop and fill the same day. A study cited by Feedipedia found that waiting a day before sealing Napier gave poor fermentation.

## Containers for a small farm

PCC lists three kinds of silo for small farms: a drum, a plastic bag, or a pit.

- Plastic drums with a tight lid. Easy to pack and rat-proof. Good for one or two head.
- Thick plastic bags or sacks. PCC's example uses bags holding about 20 to 30 kg each. They are cheap and easy to move, and you open one at a time. Watch for holes, and keep them off the floor and away from rats. In an FAO small-bag trial in Nepal, mice chewed through many bags and those bags spoiled.
- A pit or trench (bunker). Better for a bigger herd. For one animal over six months, PCC sizes a pit at 1 m deep, 3 m wide and 10 m long. Line and cover it with heavy plastic and dig it where rainwater won't flow in.

## Packing and sealing

This is where most silage succeeds or fails.

1. Fill the silo quickly, in layers.
2. Press each layer down hard. In a drum, stamp it with your feet. In a pit, walk or roll over it. PCC's silage guide suggests using a vacuum cleaner to suck air out of plastic bags (be careful not to puncture them).
3. Fill to the top so there is little empty space.
4. Seal completely so no air or rainwater gets in. Tie bags tightly, twist the neck and tie again. FAO's small-bag method puts each filled bag inside a second and third bag.
5. Put weight on top. PCC suggests old tires, wood or hollow blocks.

## Molasses

Corn usually ferments fine on its own. For Napier and other grasses, molasses gives the bacteria extra sugar. An FAO paper on tropical silage suggests about 4 to 5% of the fresh weight for tropical grasses, and Feedipedia gives about 3 to 5% for Napier. That is roughly 3 to 5 kg of molasses per 100 kg of chopped grass. Thin it with a little warm water and sprinkle it on each layer as you pack.

The same FAO paper says molasses works best on wilted grass. On very wet grass a lot of it drains away with the juice, so wilt first.

## How long to wait

PCC says silage is ready after about three weeks. FAO's small-bag guide says to wait at least a month. Either way, leave it sealed until you need it. Well-sealed silage keeps for months. In the FAO trial, bags with no damage were still good after six months.

## Good silage and spoiled silage

From PCC's training in Lupao, good silage is:

- yellowish green
- sweet and pleasantly sour in smell
- free of mold

Spoiled silage is moldy or smells rotten. Throw away the moldy parts, especially the top layer and the edges where air got in.

## Feeding it

PCC says silage can be fed to animals six months old and up. Start with a small amount mixed with the usual grass and increase over several days so the rumen gets used to it, as with any change of feed. Keep giving clean water and some dry roughage like dayami (rice straw).

Once a bag or drum is open, air starts spoiling it. PCC's advice is to keep feeding from an opened bag until it is finished. For a pit, take out only what you need each day and put the cover back right away. Open only as much as your animals can finish in a day or two.

## Moldy silage is not safe

Do not feed moldy silage. PCC warns that mold can cause diarrhea and abortion. Manitoba Agriculture adds that spoiled silage can carry listeria and molds that lower intake and growth, and that young and pregnant animals are most at risk. If an animal goes off feed or acts strangely after eating silage, stop feeding that batch and call a veterinarian or your Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO).

## Summary

- Silage is chopped green forage sealed with no air. It lets you store rainy-season grass for tag-init.
- Cut Napier at about 45 to 55 days and corn at about 75 to 80 days, then wilt wet grass for a day or two.
- Chop to 1 to 2 cm, pack hard, seal tight and put weight on top.
- Add molasses to Napier or other grass silage, roughly 3 to 5% of the fresh weight.
- Wait at least three weeks. Good silage is yellowish green, smells sweet-sour and has no mold.
- Introduce it slowly, finish opened containers quickly, and never feed moldy silage.
