Raisers spend a lot of time thinking about grass and feeds, and much less about water. But a cow that does not drink enough will not eat enough, and an animal that does not eat will not gain. In a warm country like ours, water is the first thing to check when an animal looks off.

## How much do they drink

There is no single number, because water intake depends on body weight, heat, the feed and whether a cow is carrying a calf or giving milk. The figures below come from foreign extension services, so use them as a rough guide.

Colorado State Extension gives a rule of thumb of about 2 gallons per 100 pounds of body weight on days above 90°F (about 32°C). That works out to roughly 17 liters per 100 kg. An Irish source, Teagasc, says cattle can drink up to about 15 liters per 100 kg in warm weather. So a 300 kg animal on a hot day may easily drink 45 liters or more.

Mississippi State Extension, using US research tables, lists about 5.8 gallons a day for a 400 pound growing calf at 70°F and about 9.5 gallons at 90°F. In metric, that is about 22 liters going up to about 36 liters for a calf of roughly 180 kg. The lesson is the same: the hotter it gets, the more water you need, and the amount rises faster than most people expect.

Feed matters too. Animals eating dry feed such as dayami or hay need more water than animals eating fresh, green grass, which is mostly water itself. Animals on a high-concentrate ration in the fattening period also drink more.

## Why water affects weight gain

When water is short, intake of feed drops. Weight gain drops with it. A fattener that loses a few weeks of gain because the trough ran dry costs you real money, even if nobody noticed.

Heat stress makes it worse. During the hot months before the rains, check your troughs more than once a day. (There is a separate guide on heat stress.)

## Check the quality

Clean water is not only water that looks clear. The University of Georgia reports that total dissolved solids (TDS, a measure of salts) below 3,000 ppm do not reduce gain or feed intake, while water above 5,000 ppm should not be used for cattle. Those are US figures, but the idea applies here: salty water is a problem.

If your well is near the sea, or the water tastes brackish, do not guess. Ask your MAO or the nearest state university or DA laboratory how to have it tested.

Other things to watch:

- Algae and slime. Green scum in a pond or trough can be harmful. Blue-green algae can poison livestock, especially in warm, still water, so ask your veterinarian before letting animals drink from a green pond.
- Manure and mud. Animals that stand in the water they drink from foul it with manure, which spreads parasites and disease. Liver fluke is linked to wet, swampy places, so keep animals out of stagnant pools.
- Chemicals. Water downstream of farm sprays, fertilizer runoff or fish ponds treated with chemicals can carry residues. Do not let cattle drink from them.
- Warm water. UGA notes that shallow ponds and small troughs heat up under the sun, and animals drink less from warm water. Put troughs in shade.

## Setting up troughs

You do not need anything fancy. What works:

- A trough big enough that two animals can drink without fighting. Dominant animals push others away from a small trough.
- A trough placed under a roof or a tree, away from the feed bunk, so animals do not dirty it with feed or droppings.
- A raised edge or a concrete apron so the area around it does not turn into mud.
- A simple float valve or a drum with a hose, if you have a water line. This keeps the level steady without constant carrying.
- A drain plug, so you can empty and scrub it.

Clean the trough regularly. Scrub the slime off and let the sun dry it. How often depends on how fast it dirties, but if you can see green or smell sourness, clean it today.

## When you carry water by hand

Many small raisers carry water in drums or pails. If this is you, make a schedule, because water is easy to forget when you are busy with grass. Fill the trough in the morning and again in the afternoon, and put a bit extra in during hot weather. If you tie animals, offer water at least twice a day, and when the weather is hot, more often.

Think about the source as well. In the dry season, a creek or a shallow well may shrink or dry up. Ask neighbors, your barangay and the MAO about alternatives well before it happens, for example rainwater storage from your roof during the rainy season.

## Water and your other jobs

Calves, bulls and cows that are nursing need water too, and they can be shy in a crowded kural. Give them extra space. And when you transport animals, remember that they have been off water for hours. Offer clean water as soon as they arrive, but allow them to rest first.

Never leave urea, fertilizer or chemicals where animals can mix them with their drinking water. Thirsty animals will drink anything within reach.

## Summary

- A hot day can raise a cow's water need to roughly 15 to 17 liters per 100 kg of body weight, according to extension sources abroad, so check your troughs twice a day.
- Less water means less feed eaten and less weight gained.
- Salty, algae-covered or manure-fouled water makes animals sick. Have doubtful water tested.
- Use a shaded, clean trough with enough room for several animals to drink.
- Plan for the dry season early, because springs and shallow wells can fail.
