A bull gives half the genes of every calf he sires, so a poor bull costs you for years. A good one can lift the weight of your whole calf crop. Because a bull is a big purchase, it pays to look closely before you pay.

## Decide what you need

Start by asking what the bull is for. A bull for a herd of native cows is not the same as one for crossbred cows or for pure Brahman breeding. Think about:

- The breed of your cows. Keep in mind how the offspring will do in your heat, feed supply and tick pressure.
- Your goal. Faster growth for fattening, or smaller calves for first-time heifers, or breed purity.
- Whether you need a bull at all. For a few cows, artificial insemination (AI) may cost less than keeping a bull all year. Ask your MAO or the provincial veterinary office whether AI service is available in your area.

## Look at the animal

A bull should look like a bull of his breed: strong neck and shoulders, a masculine head and a calm but active temperament. Then check the parts that matter most for work.

- Feet and legs. A bull must walk, mount and cover ground. Avoid bulls with weak pasterns, swollen joints or a stiff gait. The New Mexico State University guide notes that swollen joints can be an early sign of problems that end a bull's working life sooner.
- Body condition. Not too thin and not overfat. A bull in poor condition usually has lower fertility, and young bulls lose weight fast once they start working.
- Eyes and general health. Clear eyes, healthy coat and no signs of ticks or skin disease. Quarantine him when he arrives (see the guide on new animals).
- Testicles. Both should be present, firm and similar in size, hanging well.
- Temperament. A dangerous bull is a danger to you and your family. Do not buy one that charges people just because he looks good.

## Get a breeding soundness exam

A veterinarian can do a breeding soundness examination (BSE). US extension guides from North Dakota State University and the University of Arkansas advise doing it 30 to 60 days before each breeding season, so there is time to find another bull if he fails. The exam includes a physical check, measuring the scrotal circumference, checking the sex organs and looking at the semen under a microscope.

Scrotal circumference matters because it is linked to how much sperm a bull makes each day, according to Alabama Extension. The minimums used in the US come from the Society for Theriogenology, a group of veterinarians: about 30 cm for bulls under 15 months, rising to 34 cm for bulls over 24 months. These are foreign figures. The University of Arkansas guide notes that Brahman bulls have smaller testicles when young and mature later, though adult Brahman bulls reach sizes similar to other beef breeds. So a young Brahman or native bull may measure under the table and still turn out fine. Ask your vet what applies to your animal.

The result is usually satisfactory, deferred or unsatisfactory. Deferred often means a young bull who is not mature yet and should be checked again later.

Whether a vet near you does this exam varies by province. Ask the Provincial Veterinary Office or your nearest state university with an animal science department.

## How many cows per bull

There is no single national number. The Philippine figures available come from an older government program. DA Administrative Order No. 10 of 1998, for the Beef Cattle Genetic Improvement Program, set nucleus farm modules of 25 cows with 2 bulls, or 50 cows with 4 bulls under natural breeding. That is roughly one bull for every 12 or 13 cows. Its crossbred multiplier modules were 10 cows with 1 bull, or 25 cows with 2 bulls.

US guides allow more. NDSU Extension says a mature bull typically covers 25 to 30 cows, and a bull under two years about one cow per month of his age. Those numbers come from large US herds with a set breeding season. Treat the DA figures as a careful starting point, and ask your vet or the BAI office. A young bull should serve fewer cows than a mature one.

## Seasonal breeding

DA Administrative Order No. 14 of 1994, the guidelines for the DA's own cattle breeding program, set the beef cattle breeding season from April 16 to June 15 each year. It also called for strict culling and selection before each season. You do not have to follow those dates on your own farm. But the idea is useful: decide when you want calves, and then arrange your bull exam, cow condition and feed to fit. Calves born when grass is plentiful do better.

## Buying a bull

- Ask for papers. A pedigreed animal should come with records of its parents. Do not pay a pedigree price without them.
- Ask for performance records if they exist, like birth weight and growth. Weights and records are the only honest way to compare bulls.
- See the sire and dam, and a few of the bull's relatives, if you can.
- Look at his offspring if he is not young.
- Buy from a farm with a good health record, and ask about vaccinations and herd testing.
- Agree on a return or replacement if the bull proves infertile, and put it in writing.

Price is only part of the cost. Add transport, feed, housing, vet checks and the risk that he is not fertile. A cheap bull that does not get cows pregnant costs far more than an expensive one that does.

## Caring for a bull

Give him a strong fence and his own pen when he is not with the cows. Feed him well before the breeding season, because he can lose a lot of weight while working. Check his feet. Keep records of which cows he served and when, so you can check for pregnancies and plan calving.

Do not keep one bull too long with his own daughters. Rotate or replace him before his daughters are old enough to breed, or inbreeding can show in smaller, weaker calves.

## Summary

- Choose a bull that fits your cows, your feed and your goal, or consider AI for a small herd.
- Check feet, legs, testicles, body condition and temperament, and avoid dangerous animals.
- Ask a veterinarian for a breeding soundness exam 30 to 60 days before breeding.
- A 1998 DA program used about 1 bull for each 10 to 13 cows. Confirm with your vet.
- Buy with papers and records, and keep an eye on inbreeding.
