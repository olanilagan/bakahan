A bull gives half the genes of every calf he sires, so a poor bull costs you for years. A good one can lift the weight of your whole calf crop. Because a bull is a big purchase, it pays to look closely before you pay.

## Decide what you need

Start by asking what the bull is for. A bull for a herd of native cows is not the same as one for crossbred cows or for pure Brahman breeding. Think about:

- The breed of your cows. Keep in mind how the offspring will do in your heat, feed supply and tick pressure.
- Your goal. Faster growth for fattening, or smaller calves for first-time heifers, or breed purity.
- The question of whether you need a bull at all. For a few cows, artificial insemination (AI) through the municipal or provincial veterinary office may cost less than keeping a bull all year. Ask your MAO whether AI service is available in your area.

## Look at the animal

A bull should look like a bull of his breed: strong neck and shoulders, a masculine head and a good, active temperament. Then check the parts that matter most for work.

- Feet and legs. A bull must walk, mount and cover ground. Avoid bulls with weak pasterns, swollen joints or a stiff gait.
- Body condition. Not too thin and not overfat. Fat bulls are often poor breeders.
- Eyes and general health. Clear eyes, healthy coat and no signs of ticks or skin disease. Quarantine him when he arrives (see the guide on new animals).
- Testicles. Both should be present, firm and similar in size, hanging well.
- Temperament. A dangerous bull is a danger to you and your family. Do not buy one that charges people because he has good looks.

## Get a breeding soundness exam

A veterinarian can do a breeding soundness examination (BSE). Extension services in the US all recommend it, and they say it should be done 30 to 60 days before each breeding season. The exam includes a physical check, measuring the scrotal circumference, checking the sex organs and looking at the semen under a microscope.

Scrotal circumference matters because it is linked to how much semen a bull makes. The extension tables from Missouri, NDSU and Alabama list minimums of about 30 cm for bulls under 15 months and about 34 cm for bulls 24 months and older. These tables were made for temperate breeds, so Brahman and native bulls may differ. Ask your vet what applies to your animal.

The result is usually satisfactory, deferred or unsatisfactory. Deferred often means a young bull who is not mature yet and should be checked again.

Availability of such exams in the Philippines varies by province. Ask the Provincial Veterinary Office or your nearest state university with an animal science department.

## How many cows per bull

There is no single national number. The specific figures available come from older government programs. A 1998 DA program document sets module sizes for nucleus farms of about 25 cows with 2 bulls, or 50 cows with 4 bulls under natural breeding. That is roughly one bull for every 12 or 13 cows. A crossbred multiplier module was about 10 to 12 cows per bull. These are program guidelines from the 1990s, so treat them as a starting point and ask your vet or the BAI office. A young bull should usually serve fewer cows than a mature one.

## Seasonal breeding

DA guidance for Brahman breeding in 1994 set a breeding season in its program (April 16 to June 15 for its breeding services) and told breeders to cull and select animals before each season. You do not have to follow that schedule on your own farm. But the idea is useful: decide when you want calves, and then arrange your bull exams, cow condition and feed to fit. Calves born when grass is plentiful do better.

## Buying a bull

- Ask for papers. A pedigreed animal should come with records of its parents. Do not pay a pedigree price without them.
- Ask for performance records if they exist, like birth weight and growth. Weighing and records are the only way to compare bulls honestly.
- See the sire and dam, and a few of the bull's relatives, if you can.
- Look at his offspring if he is not young.
- Buy from a farm with a good health record, and ask about vaccinations and herd testing.
- Agree on a return or replacement if the bull proves infertile, and put it in writing.

Price is only part of the cost. Add transport, feed, housing, vet checks and the risk that he is not fertile. A cheap bull that does not get cows pregnant costs far more than an expensive one that does.

## Caring for a bull

Give him a strong fence and his own pen when he is not with the cows. Feed him well before the breeding season, because he can lose a lot of weight while working. Check his feet. Keep records of which cows he served and when, so you can check for pregnancies and plan calving.

Do not keep one bull too long with his own daughters. Rotate or replace him after a few years, or inbreeding will show in smaller, weaker calves.

## Summary

- Choose a bull that fits your cows, your feed and your goal, or consider AI for a small herd.
- Check feet, legs, testicles, body condition and temperament, and avoid dangerous animals.
- Ask a veterinarian for a breeding soundness exam 30 to 60 days before breeding.
- Older DA programs used about 1 bull for each 10 to 13 cows. Confirm with your vet.
- Buy with papers and records, and keep an eye on inbreeding.
