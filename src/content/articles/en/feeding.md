Feed is where most of your money goes in raising cattle, and it's also where you can save the most. Cattle are made to live on grass and farm leftovers that people can't eat. With some planning, you can feed them well on forage you grow yourself, crop leftovers from around the barangay, and only a little bought feed.

## How much a cow eats

As a rough guide, DA material cited by Edge Davao says cattle eat about 3% of their body weight in air-dry feed a day. For a 250 kg animal, that's around 7.5 kg of dry feed. Fresh grass is mostly water, so the fresh forage you cut will weigh several times more. Watch your animals. If the trough is always empty early and they still look hungry, give more. If a lot is left over and wasted, give less.

## Grasses

Grass should be most of the diet. Common grasses here for cut-and-carry or grazing:

- Napier (*Pennisetum purpureum*). Tall, high-yielding, good for cut-and-carry. According to a Philippine Carabao Center (PCC) article, ordinary Napier is usually cut at around 45 to 55 days of regrowth.
- Pakchong 1, or "Super Napier." An improved Napier from Thailand. In the same PCC article, a Thai expert claimed higher yields and about 16 to 18% crude protein, cut about every 45 to 48 days. Those are the developer's claims. What you get will depend on your soil, fertilizer and rain.
- Guinea grass (*Megathyrsus maximus*). Leafy and cattle like it. Good for pasture or cutting.

Cut grass while it's young and leafy, and chop it so less goes to waste. Plant enough that you don't run short in the dry season.

## Legumes

Legume trees and shrubs give protein, which grass alone often lacks. Two of the most common on our farms:

### Ipil-ipil (*Leucaena leucocephala*)

A very good protein source, and the base of the traditional Batangas *supak* feeding. But it contains mimosine, which can be toxic when fed in large amounts for a long time. Feedipedia, an FAO-supported feed database, notes that leucaena can harm cattle when it's more than about 30% of the diet over long periods. For most raisers, it's safer to use ipil-ipil as an add-on, not the main feed. If you see hair loss, drooling or poor appetite, call your vet.

### Kakawate or madre de cacao (*Gliricidia sepium*)

Often planted as a living fence. Feedipedia says it's best used as a protein supplement for cattle eating grass, straw or silage, not as the only feed. Studies cited there used it at around 15 to 45% of the diet in different situations, and no single safe limit is given. From the same source:

- Animals that aren't used to kakawate may refuse it at first, so introduce it slowly.
- Wilting the leaves for about a day makes cattle more willing to eat them.
- Don't suddenly give a lot of very young, lush leaves.

## Crop leftovers

- Rice straw (*dayami*). Plenty of it after harvest, but low in protein and hard to digest. It works best with green forage, legumes or a supplement. Philippine studies have tried treating straw with urea or spraying it with urea-molasses to improve it. Get training before you try this, because urea is dangerous (see below).
- Corn stover, the stalks and leaves left after the corn harvest. Like straw, it's poor feed on its own. In a Tarlac trial, cattle fed only corn stover gained just about 85 to 130 grams a day.
- Sugarcane tops (*pang-ibabaw ng tubo*). Widely used in sugar areas. A UPLB study in Negros Occidental found farm diets were about three-fourths roughage, mostly cane tops with fresh ipil-ipil, and about one-fourth concentrate.
- Sweet potato vines (*talbos ng kamote*). In a PCARRD-supported project in Paniqui, Tarlac, sweet potato vines and leaves were the main feed in an improved fattening system that pushed daily gains well above what farmers saw before.

## Silage

*Photo: Tractorboy60, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons. Colour adjusted.*

Silage is chopped green forage, often corn, packed tight with no air so it ferments and keeps for months. It lets you feed well when grass is scarce.

From PCC and PCAARRD material on corn silage:

- Forage corn is usually harvested at around 75 to 80 days, when the kernel is at about two-thirds milk line.
- Chop it fine, pack it tight into plastic sacks, silo bags or pits, squeeze out the air and seal it well. Air left inside causes mold.
- Silage is usually ready after about 3 weeks.
- Good silage is yellowish green and smells sweet and pleasantly sour. Don't feed moldy or rotten-smelling silage.

Start your animals on silage slowly. Once you open a bag, use it up fast before it spoils.

## Molasses and urea-molasses mineral blocks (UMMB)

Molasses is the sweet syrup left over from sugarcane. It makes dry feeds like straw taste better and adds energy.

Urea-molasses mineral blocks are hard licks made of molasses, urea, minerals and a binder. Urea gives the microbes in the rumen nitrogen to build protein, which helps animals that are eating poor roughage.

### Be careful with urea

Too much urea at once can kill cattle. FAO guidance says:

- Give blocks only to ruminants (cattle, carabao, goats, sheep). Never to pigs or horses.
- Use them as a supplement, not the main feed. The animal must have enough roughage in its rumen.
- Don't give blocks to very thin, hungry animals with empty stomachs.
- Introduce them over 1 to 2 weeks, starting with only 2 to 4 hours of access a day.
- Blocks must be hard. Soft or wet blocks let animals eat too much. Keep them out of the rain.
- Cattle should eat roughly 400 to 800 grams of block a day.
- Never leave urea fertilizer, or urea mixed in water, where animals can reach it. Many poisonings came from thirsty animals drinking urea solution.

Signs of poisoning are hard breathing, trembling, heavy drooling, staggering and bloating, sometimes within about 20 minutes. Call a vet right away. FAO describes giving diluted vinegar by mouth as first aid, but do this with a vet's guidance.

If you're not sure, buy ready-made blocks from a supplier you trust and follow the label.

## Concentrates

Concentrates are feeds packed with energy or protein: rice bran (*darak*), copra meal, corn, commercial cattle feeds and the like. They speed up gains but cost more.

Start with a little and increase slowly over a couple of weeks. Concentrates go on top of forage, not in place of it. Compare what they cost with the extra weight you get.

## Water, salt and minerals

- Clean drinking water should be there all the time. Cattle eat less and grow slower when water runs short.
- Give salt and a mineral mix or mineral block, especially if your animals eat mostly straw or grass from poor soil.
- Clean the troughs regularly.

## A simple example ration

This table is only an example of how feeds can fit together for a feeder of around 250 kg in a backyard setup. It's not a formula. Your amounts depend on your animal's weight, the feeds you have, and advice from your Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO) or a nutritionist.

| Feed | Example amount per day | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh chopped napier or guinea grass | Most of the diet, offered freely | Main roughage |
| Fresh ipil-ipil or wilted kakawate leaves | A few kilos, kept as a minority of the diet | Protein |
| Rice straw or corn stover | As extra roughage when grass is short | Bulk |
| Rice bran or other concentrate | Small amount, increased slowly | Energy for faster gain |
| Molasses | A little, mixed into dry feeds | Taste and energy |
| Salt and minerals | Free access via mineral block | Health |
| Clean water | Always available | Essential |

Weigh your animals regularly and adjust.

## Summary

- Grass and legumes from your own farm should be the base of the diet. Bought concentrates support forage, they don't replace it.
- Use ipil-ipil as an add-on. Feedipedia notes problems above about 30% of the diet over long periods. Wilt kakawate and introduce it slowly.
- Silage and crop leftovers get you through the dry season. Add legumes or a supplement when the roughage is poor.
- Urea can kill cattle. Use hard blocks, introduce them slowly, never give them to starved animals, and never leave urea or urea water within reach.
- Always give clean water, salt and minerals, and ask your MAO or vet for a ration that fits your farm.
