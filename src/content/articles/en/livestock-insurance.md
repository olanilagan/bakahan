One cow can be a year of savings. If it dies from disease, drowning or a lightning strike, a small raiser can lose more than he can recover. The Philippine Crop Insurance Corporation (PCIC), a government corporation under the DA, offers livestock insurance. Details change, so use this guide to know what to ask, and confirm the current terms at your nearest PCIC office.

## What it covers

PCIC's livestock insurance is mortality insurance. It pays when an insured animal dies of a covered cause. Cattle and carabao are included, along with horses, swine, goats and sheep, and poultry under commercial cover.

PCIC's program profile, dated December 2020, lists these covered risks for the non-commercial cover: diseases; accidents such as drowning, strangulation and snakebite; fire and lightning; and accidents while moving the animal to and from a place of treatment. It excludes deaths from vehicular accidents. The full list of covered causes and exclusions is in the policy. Ask PCIC for a copy and read the section on what is not covered.

Insurance does not pay if an animal is simply sold at a loss or loses weight.

## Who can apply and for which animals

A copy of PCIC's livestock guidelines posted by the Polillo, Quezon LGU lists cattle and carabao as insurable from 7 months to 5 years of age. The 2020 program profile adds that animals over 5 but not over 17 years old can be accepted for an additional premium. These details may have been updated, so confirm them.

The profile lists two kinds of cover: non-commercial and commercial. The non-commercial cover is the one aimed at small raisers. Ask which one applies to your herd.

## What it costs

The 2020 profile lists premium rates for non-commercial cattle and carabao cover of 5.0 to 6.5 percent of the sum insured, and 5 to 10 percent for commercial cover. The same profile shows the amount of cover for non-commercial cattle and carabao at only ₱7,000 to ₱15,000 per head, which is far below what a cow sells for today. Ask PCIC for today's rate table and the highest amount you can insure each animal for.

An example, using made-up round numbers: if a fattener is insured for ₱15,000 and the rate is 5 percent, the premium would be ₱750. Your actual rate and amount will be different.

## Free coverage for some farmers

The government pays the premium for farmers and fisherfolk listed in the Registry System for Basic Sectors in Agriculture (RSBSA). According to the DA, this free program continues in 2026 with a bigger budget, and livestock is one of its seven product lines. Coconut farmers in the National Coconut Farmers Registry System also get free cover. Being on the list does not insure your animals by itself. You still need to enroll them, and there may be a limit on how many head are covered, so ask your MAO or PCIC. If you are not yet in the RSBSA, register through your MAO, because many government programs, including loans, start from that list.

Farmers who are not covered by the subsidy can still buy PCIC insurance at regular premiums.

## How to apply

The steps differ a little by region, but the usual order is:

- Go to the PCIC regional or provincial office, or ask your MAO to connect you with PCIC.
- Bring a valid ID, your RSBSA registration if you have one, and the details of your animals.
- The animals are inspected and identified, often with an ear tag. Ask what PCIC requires.
- You choose a sum insured within the allowed range and pay the premium unless it is subsidized.
- You receive a policy. Keep it with your records.

## Make a claim that will be accepted

PCIC's exact claim rules for livestock, including how many days you have to report a death, could not be confirmed for this guide. Ask PCIC for the claim rules when you enroll, and write them down. Until you know the rules, plan on the following.

- Report the death as soon as you can. Ask PCIC what the deadline is, and keep their phone number where you can find it.
- Do not bury or dispose of the carcass until PCIC or a veterinarian has seen it, unless the disease risk requires it. Ask them what to do.
- Keep the ear tag or identification.
- Take clear photos of the dead animal and the tag.
- Ask the veterinarian for a written statement of the cause of death.
- Keep the policy and receipts.

If the death looks like a disease that must be reported, such as one with blisters in the mouth and feet, tell the veterinarian and BAI first.

## Insurance and loans

Lenders sometimes want insurance. A cattle loan for out-of-school youth launched in Ilocos in December 2022, through the DA's Agricultural Credit Policy Council, came with full PCIC livestock insurance on the animals and required a cattle-raising training. If you plan to borrow for cattle, ask whether PCIC cover is needed and add the premium to your budget.

## Is it worth it

For a small raiser who pays the premium, it is a fixed cost that comes out of the margin. Insurance does not prevent losses, so good care still comes first. But for a larger herd, or for a first-time raiser whose savings are in one or two animals, protection against a sudden loss can be worth the cost. Do the math with your own numbers, compare it to the money you could lose, and decide.

## Summary

- PCIC livestock insurance pays when an insured animal dies of a covered cause, such as disease, drowning or lightning. Ask for the full list of covered causes and exclusions.
- Older PCIC documents list cattle as insurable from 7 months to 5 years, at premiums of about 5 to 6.5 percent for non-commercial cover. Confirm current rates and the maximum amount per head.
- RSBSA-listed farmers can get free premiums under the 2026 program, but the animals still need to be enrolled.
- Report any death quickly, keep the tag, take photos and get a vet statement.
- Add the premium to your cost computation and compare it with the loss you could not afford.
