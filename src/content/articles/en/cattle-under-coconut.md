Coconut lands cover a large part of the country, and the ground under the trees is often only weeds and shade. Putting cattle there is an old idea, and the Philippine Coconut Authority (PCA) has recommended livestock as an added source of income for coconut farmers. It is not free money, though. Done badly, it can hurt both the palms and the animals.

## Why it works

There are two main reasons. First, cattle eat the weeds and grass under the palms, which saves on brushing. The FAO book on pasture-cattle-coconut systems says the benefit depends on the weeding costs saved, especially in larger plantations. Business Diary reports the same: weeds are forage for the animals, so you save on weeding.

Second, you earn from the animals while you wait for the next coconut harvest. Coconut income comes in cycles. A fattener or a breeding cow adds another source of cash.

A Western Mindanao study reported that adding animals raised the farmers' profit by up to PhP 47,027 per hectare per year, which is one case from 2009. Treat it as an example of what is possible and not as a promise. Your own result depends on prices, feed, animal health and how well you manage.

## Pasture under the palms

Grass under coconuts gets less sun, so not every grass grows well. Studies in the Philippines have used signalgrass (*Brachiaria decumbens*) under coconuts. A study from Ligao, Albay tested signalgrass in rotational grazing and cut-and-carry forages on smallholder coconut farms and reported clear benefits. Ask your MAO or PCA office which grasses and legumes do well in your area, because soil, rainfall and tree spacing differ.

Business Diary also cautions that high-yielding grasses and legumes can compete with the palms for nutrients, so the pasture should balance what both the palms and the animals need. Palms need fertilizer too, and you should keep up your usual program.

## How many animals

Stocking rate means how many animals you put on a hectare, usually counted in animal units (AU, roughly one mature cow). Too many animals ruin the pasture and the soil.

A southern Luzon trial with signalgrass under coconuts, cited in the CORE document, found that total liveweight gain per hectare was highest at 3 AU per hectare. But the best financial return was at 2 AU per hectare. The FAO book cites a view that about 1 to 1.5 livestock equivalents per hectare was enough for reasonable control of weeds and brush.

So the numbers are a range, not a rule. A sensible approach is to start low, such as one or two head per hectare, and watch your pasture. If the grass is being eaten to the ground, you have too many.

## Protect the young palms

Cattle and young coconuts do not mix. Animals can trample seedlings, chew the leaves or damage the base of the trunk. Keep cattle out of newly planted areas until the palms are tall enough that the growing point is out of reach. Ask the PCA office what height is safe in your area.

Cattle also need to stay off areas where you have just planted new forage until it has rooted.

## Shade is useful, and so is water

The shade under the palms is a plus in hot weather. Cattle under a canopy have less heat stress than cattle on open grass. But shade does not replace water. Put a trough in the shade, and check it daily.

Dense shade from old palms can slow the growth of the grass underneath. As the palms age or the canopy thickens, the pasture may weaken. Be ready to supplement with cut-and-carry forage or to change the plan.

## Choose the right animals

For a smallholder, fattening two or three animals under the palms is easier to start with than a breeding herd. Native cattle and tropical crosses do well in heat and with rough feed. ATI-Central Luzon, PCA and BAI held a coconut-cattle farming training in Aurora in April 2026, and those agencies are the first place to ask about training near you.

You still need the basics: housing at night, records, deworming and vaccines under a vet's advice, and a plan for the dry season. All of these are covered in our other guides.

## Government help

PCA has been a partner in a DA livestock dispersal program for coconut farmers, according to the Philippine News Agency. The program reported in that article targets coconut farmers who are leaseholders or tenants on up to five hectares, and it focuses on sheep. Programs change from year to year and by province. Ask the PCA provincial office and your MAO what is available, and what you have to qualify for.

## Do the sums first

Before you buy animals, work out your costs and expected sale. Include the animals, fencing, water, forage planting, vet costs, labor and transport. Then compare it with what you earn now from the land. If the numbers are thin, start smaller.

## Summary

- Cattle under coconuts eat weeds, which can cut weeding costs, and give you income between harvests.
- Choose shade-tolerant pasture grasses and ask your MAO or PCA which ones fit your area.
- A southern Luzon trial found 2 AU per hectare gave the best return, and 3 AU gave the most total gain.
- Keep animals away from young palms and from newly planted forage.
- Provide water and shade, and compute your costs before you buy.
