Coconut lands cover a large part of the country, and the ground under the trees is often only weeds and shade. Putting cattle there is an old idea, and Philippine Coconut Authority (PCA) officials have long urged coconut farmers to add livestock as another source of income. It is not free money, though. Done badly, it can hurt both the palms and the animals.

## Why it works

There are two main reasons. First, cattle eat the weeds and grass under the palms, which saves on brushing. The FAO book *Pasture-Cattle-Coconut Systems* (Reynolds, 1995) cites a Philippine author who found that the advantage of adding livestock depends on how much you save on weeding and herbicide.

Second, you earn from the animals while you wait for the next coconut harvest. Coconut income comes in cycles. A fattener or a breeding cow adds another source of cash.

A DA research project in Western Mindanao reported in 2009 that adding animals raised farmers' profit by up to PhP 47,027 per hectare per year. That is the best result from one project, not an average. Treat it as an example of what is possible and not as a promise. Your own result depends on prices, feed, animal health and how well you manage.

## Pasture under the palms

Grass under coconuts gets less sun, so not every grass grows well. A Bureau of Animal Industry (BAI) team led by F. A. Moog grew signalgrass (*Brachiaria decumbens*) under established coconuts on a smallholder farm in Ligao, Albay, and grazed cattle on it. The same team also followed farmers who fed cut-and-carry forage, and found that results varied with how much and how good the feed was, and how diligent the farmer was. Ask your MAO or PCA office which grasses and legumes do well in your area, because soil, rainfall and tree spacing differ.

Business Diary also cautions that improved grasses and legumes compete with the palms for soil nutrients. Palms need fertilizer too, so keep up your usual program.

## How many animals

Stocking rate means how many animals you put on a hectare, usually counted in animal units (AU, roughly one mature cow). Too many animals compact the soil and damage both the trees and the pasture, according to the FAO book.

In the Ligao trial, the BAI team compared 1, 2 and 3 AU per hectare. Grazing at 2 or 3 AU gave much more liveweight gain per hectare than 1 AU, but 2 AU per hectare gave the best financial return. That was improved signalgrass, not native grass.

The FAO book gives a wider picture. How many animals a hectare carries depends mostly on how much light reaches the ground. Where less than half the sunlight gets through, expect less than 1 AU per hectare. Only with more than 80% light does it approach 2 AU. Native pasture under closely spaced palms may carry as little as 0.25 AU per hectare. For weed control alone, the book cites about 1 to 1.5 animals per hectare.

So the numbers are a range, not a rule. A sensible approach is to start low, such as one head per hectare, and watch your pasture. If the grass is being eaten to the ground, you have too many.

## Protect the young palms

Cattle and young coconuts do not mix. Animals can trample seedlings, chew the leaves or damage the base of the trunk. Keep cattle out of newly planted areas until the palms are tall enough that the leaves and growing point are out of reach. Ask the PCA office what age or height is safe in your area.

Cattle also need to stay off areas where you have just planted new forage until it has rooted.

## Shade is useful, and so is water

The shade under the palms is a plus in hot weather, because cattle can rest out of the sun. But shade does not replace water. Put a trough in the shade, and check it daily.

Dense shade slows the growth of the grass underneath. As the canopy thickens, the pasture may weaken. Be ready to supplement with cut-and-carry forage or to reduce the number of animals.

## Choose the right animals

For a smallholder, fattening two or three animals under the palms is easier to start with than a breeding herd. Native cattle and tropical crosses do well in heat and with rough feed. ATI-Central Luzon, PCA and BAI held a three-day coconut-cattle farming training for coconut farmers in Aurora in April 2026. Those agencies are the first place to ask about training near you.

You still need the basics: housing at night, records, deworming and vaccines under a vet's advice, and a plan for the dry season. All of these are covered in our other guides.

## Government help

The Coconut Farmers and Industry Development Plan (CFIDP), funded by the coco levy trust fund, has a native cattle component run by BAI with PCA. In April 2026, for example, coconut farmers in BARMM trained in native cattle raising under CFIDP were expected to plant pasture before receiving cattle from BAI. To join CFIDP programs you generally need to be listed in the National Coconut Farmers Registry System (NCFRS). Programs change from year to year and by province. Ask the PCA provincial office and your MAO what is open, and what you need to qualify.

## Do the sums first

Before you buy animals, work out your costs and expected sale. Include the animals, fencing, water, forage planting, vet costs, labor and transport. Then compare it with what you earn now from the land. If the numbers are thin, start smaller.

## Summary

- Cattle under coconuts eat weeds, which can cut weeding costs, and give you income between harvests.
- How many animals a hectare carries depends on how much light reaches the grass. Start low and watch the pasture.
- In a BAI trial in Ligao, Albay, 2 AU per hectare on signalgrass gave the best financial return.
- Keep animals away from young palms and from newly planted forage.
- If you are in the NCFRS, ask PCA about CFIDP cattle training and dispersal, and compute your costs before you buy.
