Fattening cattle is a business, and you make or lose money on the numbers. The math is simple. Once you know how cattle are priced and where your money goes, you can tell whether a deal makes sense before you hand over your puhunan (capital).

## How cattle are priced

Cattle usually change hands in one of three ways here.

### Per kilo liveweight

The animal is weighed alive and paid for at so many pesos per kilo. This is the fairest way for both sides, because everyone can see the scale. The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) reports farmgate prices of cattle for slaughter per kilogram liveweight in its quarterly Cattle Situation Reports. Check these to get a feel for price levels and trends in your region.

### Per head (by eye)

Buyer and seller agree on one price for the whole animal, usually after guessing its weight by eye. It's quick, but whoever guesses better wins. If you buy or sell per head, weigh the animal or at least use a weight tape, so you know what you are really paying per kilo.

### Per kilo carcass (dressed weight)

Some buyers, especially slaughterhouses and meat traders, pay by the weight of the carcass. That is the body after slaughter, with the head, hide, feet and internal organs removed. The price per kilo is higher than liveweight, because you are only paying for meat and bone.

## Dressing percentage

Dressing percentage tells you how much of the live animal ends up as carcass:

> Dressing % = (Carcass weight ÷ Liveweight) × 100

For example, a 340 kg animal that gives a 170 kg carcass has a dressing percentage of 50%.

What is normal?

- For national meat supply statistics, the PSA and the Technical Working Group on Livestock and Poultry Statistics use a carcass conversion rate of 50% for cattle and carabao.
- For grain-fed feedlot cattle in the United States, the University of Nebraska says carcass weight is about 60 to 64% of live weight.
- Cattle fed mostly grass, hay or silage usually dress lower than grain-fed cattle. It also varies with fatness, breed, sex, how full the gut is and how far the animal was hauled.

So if a buyer offers a carcass price, convert it to liveweight before you compare it with other offers. A high carcass price on an animal that dresses poorly can be worth less than a fair liveweight price.

## Main costs

| Cost item | What it includes |
|---|---|
| Feeder animal | Purchase price of the young animal you will fatten, usually the biggest single cost |
| Feeds | Forage, concentrates, supplements, salt, minerals, molasses |
| Labor | Your own time or a hired caretaker for feeding, cleaning, watching animals |
| Animal health | Vaccination, deworming, vet visits, tick control, medicines |
| Transport | Hauling feeders in and finished animals out |
| Permits and papers | Veterinary Health Certificate, shipping permit, other local fees |
| Housing and equipment | Pens, roofing, feed and water troughs, scale or weight tape (spread over several batches) |
| Interest and insurance | Cost of borrowed money, livestock insurance premium |

DA guidance says you can keep feed costs down with farm by-products like dayami (rice straw), corn stover, sugarcane tops, copra meal and rice bran. It also says feeder stock is usually cheaper in the dry season (January to April).

## A simple worked example

**These are example numbers only.** They are round figures to show the formula, not current prices. Put in your own local prices before you decide anything.

DA guidance says feeder cattle are usually fattened for 120 to 180 days, reaching about 275 to 325 kg. Our example uses one animal for 150 days.

**Step 1. Income**

- Buy a feeder at 250 kg
- Example gain: 0.6 kg per day × 150 days = 90 kg
- Selling weight: 250 + 90 = 340 kg
- Example selling price: ₱200 per kg liveweight
- Income: 340 × ₱200 = ₱68,000

**Step 2. Costs**

| Example cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Feeder: 250 kg × ₱200/kg | ₱50,000 |
| Feeds: ₱60/day × 150 days | ₱9,000 |
| Labor (share per head) | ₱3,000 |
| Vet, vaccines, deworming | ₱1,000 |
| Transport and permits | ₱1,500 |
| Housing upkeep, interest, other | ₱1,000 |
| **Total cost** | **₱65,500** |

**Step 3. Profit**

> Profit = Income − Total cost = ₱68,000 − ₱65,500 = ₱2,500 per head

**Step 4. Break-even selling price**

> Break-even price = Total cost ÷ Selling weight = ₱65,500 ÷ 340 kg ≈ ₱193 per kg

This last number is the one to remember. If the selling price drops below about ₱193 per kilo, this animal loses money. A drop of just ₱10 per kilo at selling time (₱190 × 340 = ₱64,600) turns the ₱2,500 profit into a ₱900 loss.

So the margin per head can be thin. Your profit comes from the weight the animal gains and the gap between your buying and selling price, and keeping feed costs low matters a lot.

## The main risks

- **Deaths.** One dead animal can wipe out the profit from several others. Good health care is your first protection (see our health guide).
- **Price swings.** The price when you sell may be lower than when you bought. Watch PSA price reports and the local livestock markets, and know your break-even price.
- **Slow growth.** Poor feed, worms or stress mean fewer kilos to sell for the same costs.
- **Theft.** Cattle are valuable and easy to move. Secure your pens, keep watch at night, use ear tags or other marks, and keep your papers in order.
- **Disease outbreaks and movement bans.** These can stop you from buying or selling for a while.

Insurance helps. The Philippine Crop Insurance Corporation (PCIC) offers mortality insurance for cattle and carabao, for both non-commercial and commercial raisers. Ask your local PCIC office or agriculture office about current terms and what is covered.

## Keeping records

Without records, you are guessing. For every animal, write down:

- date bought, from whom, weight and price
- weights during fattening, if you can weigh
- all feed bought and what it cost
- every vaccination, deworming and treatment, with date and cost
- labor, transport and permit costs
- date sold, to whom, weight, price and total received

A plain notebook is enough. After one or two batches, your records will show your real cost per kilo gained. That number is worth more than any example in an article.

## Where to borrow puhunan

These government-linked options are worth asking about. Terms change, so always confirm with the lender.

- **ACPC Agri-Negosyo Loan Program (ANYO).** The Agricultural Credit Policy Council lends through partner lenders like cooperatives, cooperative banks and rural banks. Small farmers registered in the RSBSA (Registry System for Basic Sectors in Agriculture) can borrow up to ₱300,000. Micro and small enterprises and farmer organizations can borrow more. ACPC lists 2% interest plus a service fee, with repayment based on the project's cash flow, up to 5 years. You apply at acpcaccess.ph.
- **DA and ACPC cattle loans through provincial veterinarians.** In 2022, DA in the Ilocos Region announced loans for out-of-school youth to buy cattle for fattening, with livestock insurance through PCIC and required training in cattle raising. Ask your provincial veterinarian if a similar program is open in your area.
- **LANDBANK through cooperatives.** LANDBANK lends to accredited cooperatives for crop, livestock and fishery production, among other things. Joining a well-run cooperative can open this door.
- **Cooperatives and rural banks** near you. They are often the partner lenders for government programs.

For almost all of these, the first step is to **register in the RSBSA** at your city or municipal agriculture office.

## Summary

- Know how you are being paid: per kilo liveweight, per head, or per kilo carcass. Convert everything to per kilo liveweight before comparing.
- Dressing % = carcass weight ÷ liveweight × 100. The PSA uses 50% for cattle in national statistics. Grain-fed cattle can go higher.
- The feeder animal is usually your biggest cost, then feeds. Farm by-products help keep feed costs down.
- Work out your break-even selling price before you buy.
- Guard against deaths, price swings and theft with good care, records and insurance.
- ACPC, cooperatives, rural banks and LANDBANK lend to raisers. Register in the RSBSA first.
