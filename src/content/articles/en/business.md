Cattle fattening is a business, and like any business, it is won or lost on the numbers. The good news is that the math is simple. Once you understand how cattle are priced and where your money goes, you can tell whether a deal makes sense *before* you hand over your puhunan.

## How cattle are priced

There are three common ways cattle change hands in the Philippines.

### Per kilo liveweight

The animal is weighed alive, and the price is so many pesos per kilo. This is the fairest and most transparent method for both sides, because everyone can see the scale. The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) reports farmgate prices of cattle for slaughter **per kilogram liveweight** in its quarterly Cattle Situation Reports. Check these to get a feel for price levels and trends in your region.

### Per head (by eye)

The buyer and seller agree on one price for the whole animal, usually by estimating its weight by eye. It is quick, but the person who guesses better wins. If you buy or sell per head, weigh the animal or at least use a weight tape so you know what you are really paying per kilo.

### Per kilo carcass (dressed weight)

Some buyers, especially slaughterhouses and meat traders, pay based on the weight of the **carcass**: the body after slaughter, with the head, hide, feet and internal organs removed. The price per kilo is higher than liveweight, because you are paying only for the meat-and-bone part.

## Dressing percentage: the link between live and carcass

**Dressing percentage** tells you how much of the live animal ends up as carcass:

> Dressing % = (Carcass weight ÷ Liveweight) × 100

For example, a 340 kg animal that gives a 170 kg carcass has a dressing percentage of 50%.

How high is it, typically?

- For national meat supply statistics, the PSA and the Technical Working Group on Livestock and Poultry Statistics use a carcass conversion rate of **50%** for cattle and carabao.
- In grain-fed feedlot cattle in the United States, the University of Nebraska says carcass weight is about **60 to 64%** of live weight.
- Cattle on mostly grass, hay or silage tend to have a **lower** dressing percentage than grain-fed cattle. It also varies with fatness, breed, sex, gut fill and how far the animal was transported.

Why this matters: if a buyer offers a carcass price, convert it to liveweight before comparing it with other offers. A high carcass price on an animal that dresses poorly may be worth less than a fair liveweight price.

## Where your money goes: main cost items

| Cost item | What it includes |
|---|---|
| Feeder animal | Purchase price of the young animal you will fatten, usually the biggest single cost |
| Feeds | Forage, concentrates, supplements, salt, minerals, molasses |
| Labor | Your own time or a hired caretaker for feeding, cleaning, watching animals |
| Animal health | Vaccination, deworming, vet visits, tick control, medicines |
| Transport | Hauling feeders in and finished animals out |
| Permits and papers | Veterinary Health Certificate, shipping permit, other local fees |
| Housing and equipment | Pens, roofing, feed and water troughs, scale or weight tape (spread over several batches) |
| Interest and insurance | Cost of borrowed money, livestock insurance premium |

Department of Agriculture guidance points out that feed costs can be kept down by using farm by-products like rice straw, corn stover, sugarcane tops, copra meal and rice bran. It also says feeder stock usually comes cheaper in the dry season (January to April).

## A simple worked example

The numbers below are **EXAMPLE NUMBERS ONLY**. They are round figures to show the formula. They are **not** current prices. Plug in your own local prices before deciding anything.

DA guidance says feeder cattle are usually fattened for **120 to 180 days**, reaching about 275 to 325 kg. Our example uses one animal for 150 days.

**Step 1 – Income**

- Buy a feeder at 250 kg
- Example gain: 0.6 kg per day × 150 days = 90 kg
- Selling weight: 250 + 90 = **340 kg**
- Example selling price: ₱200 per kg liveweight
- Income: 340 × ₱200 = **₱68,000**

**Step 2 – Costs**

| Example cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Feeder: 250 kg × ₱200/kg | ₱50,000 |
| Feeds: ₱60/day × 150 days | ₱9,000 |
| Labor (share per head) | ₱3,000 |
| Vet, vaccines, deworming | ₱1,000 |
| Transport and permits | ₱1,500 |
| Housing upkeep, interest, other | ₱1,000 |
| **Total cost** | **₱65,500** |

**Step 3 – Profit**

> Profit = Income − Total cost = ₱68,000 − ₱65,500 = **₱2,500 per head**

**Step 4 – Break-even selling price**

> Break-even price = Total cost ÷ Selling weight = ₱65,500 ÷ 340 kg ≈ **₱193 per kg**

This last number is the most useful one. If the selling price drops below about ₱193/kg, this example animal loses money. A drop of just ₱10/kg at selling time (₱190 × 340 = ₱64,600) turns the ₱2,500 profit into a ₱900 loss.

The lessons: margins per head can be thin, **weight gain and the buy–sell price difference drive your profit**, and keeping feed costs low matters a lot.

## The main risks

- **Mortality.** One dead animal can wipe out the profit of several others. Good health management (see our health guide) is your first protection.
- **Price swings.** The price when you sell may be lower than when you bought. Watch PSA price reports and local auction markets, and know your break-even price.
- **Slow growth.** Poor feed, parasites or stress mean fewer kilos to sell for the same costs.
- **Theft.** Cattle are valuable and easy to move. Secure pens, watch at night, keep ear tags or other identification, and keep your papers in order.
- **Disease outbreaks and movement restrictions**, which can stop you from buying or selling for a time.

**Insurance helps.** The Philippine Crop Insurance Corporation (PCIC) offers mortality insurance cover for cattle and carabao, both for non-commercial and commercial raisers. Ask your local PCIC office or agriculture office about current terms and what is covered.

## Keep records: your business memory

Without records, you are guessing. For every animal, write down:

- Date bought, from whom, weight and price
- Weights during fattening (if you can weigh)
- All feed bought and its cost
- Every vaccination, deworming and treatment, with date and cost
- Labor, transport and permit costs
- Date sold, to whom, weight, price and total received

A simple notebook works. After one or two batches, your records will tell you your real cost per kilo gained, which is worth more than any example in an article.

## Financing options

If you need puhunan, here are government-linked options to ask about. Terms change, so always confirm directly.

- **ACPC Agri-Negosyo Loan Program (ANYO).** The Agricultural Credit Policy Council lends through partner lenders such as cooperatives, cooperative banks and rural banks. Individual small farmers registered in the **RSBSA** (Registry System for Basic Sectors in Agriculture) can borrow up to ₱300,000; micro and small enterprises and farmer organizations can borrow more. ACPC lists 2% interest plus a service fee, with repayment based on the project's cash flow, up to 5 years. Applications start at acpcaccess.ph.
- **DA–ACPC cattle loans through provincial veterinarians.** In 2022, DA in the Ilocos Region announced loans for out-of-school youth to buy cattle fatteners, with livestock insurance through PCIC and required training in cattle raising. Check with your provincial veterinarian whether a similar program is open in your area.
- **LANDBANK through cooperatives.** LANDBANK lends to accredited cooperatives for crop, livestock and fishery production, among other purposes. Joining a well-run cooperative can open this door.
- **Cooperatives and rural banks** in your area, which often serve as the partner lenders for government programs.

A first step for almost all of these: **register in the RSBSA** through your city or municipal agriculture office.

## Key takeaways

- Know how you are being paid: per kilo liveweight, per head, or per kilo carcass. Convert everything to a per-kilo liveweight basis to compare.
- Dressing % = carcass weight ÷ liveweight × 100. PSA uses 50% for cattle in national statistics; grain-fed cattle can go higher.
- The feeder animal is usually your biggest cost; feeds come next. Farm by-products help keep feed costs down.
- Always compute your break-even selling price before you buy.
- Protect against mortality, price swings and theft with good care, records and insurance.
- Financing exists through ACPC, cooperatives, rural banks and LANDBANK; register in RSBSA first.
